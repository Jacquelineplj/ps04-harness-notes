ActionOPD: Distilling Decisions, Not Tokens
Abstract
Distilling capable language-model agents into smaller models requires aligning the student’s behavior with the teacher’s. This raises a fundamental question: at which states and at what granularity should this alignment be performed? Offline imitation provides dense but token-specific supervision and can suffer from state-distribution mismatch. Token-level on-policy distillation (OPD) addresses this mismatch but typically requires teacher token probabilities and compatible tokenization. We argue that semantic actions provide a natural unit of supervision for agent distillation. We introduce ActionOPD, an action-level on-policy distillation method motivated by maximizing expected teacher-action likelihood at student-encountered states. At each such state, ActionOPD samples actions from both the student and a black-box teacher, rewarding student actions according to their semantic agreement with the sampled teacher actions. Through policy optimization, ActionOPD aligns the student’s action-level behavior with the teacher’s decisions without requiring reproduction of teacher-written reasoning or access to teacher token probabilities. Training Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B with two teachers on diverse, curated tool-use tasks yields average gains of 3.3–6.9 percentage points across four out-of-distribution benchmarks. ActionOPD scales favorably with student size and teacher capability, without requiring the teacher and student to share a model family or tokenizer. On \(\tau^{2}\)-bench, ActionOPD achieves competitive performance against offline distillation and token-level OPD. We further explore Self-ActionOPD with a same-model teacher and privileged context. These results establish ActionOPD as a promising approach to agent distillation.
1 Introduction
Language models are increasingly deployed as agents that communicate with users, maintain state, and act through external tools (Yao et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2025). In these interactions, an agent’s decisions shape subsequent observations and the states it encounters. Distilling capable but expensive frontier agents into smaller models therefore raises two questions: at which states should the teacher supervise the student, and at what granularity should their behavior be aligned?
Distillation methods address these questions differently. Offline distillation provides dense supervision by fitting teacher-generated sequences (Kim and Rush, 2016), but can face teacher–student state-distribution mismatch (Ross et al., 2011). Token-level on-policy distillation (OPD) supervises student-generated sequences using teacher token probabilities, reducing mismatch while typically requiring compatible tokenization (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). Both supervise tokens without explicitly matching action semantics (Figure 2).
Our key observation is that semantic actions provide a natural unit of supervision for agent distillation. A tool call expresses a decision through its tool name and arguments; a natural-language response conveys communicative intent and information. The same decision can be realized through different wording and reasoning. This shifts the objective from reproducing a particular teacher sequence to increasing the likelihood of its underlying action.
We introduce ActionOPD, an action-level on-policy distillation method motivated by maximizing expected teacher-action likelihood at student-encountered states. Here, likelihood is the total probability mass of student outputs that semantically match a teacher action, not the probability of reproducing one teacher-written sequence. This objective yields a REINFORCE-form policy gradient with action-level semantic agreement as reward (Williams, 1992). In practice, ActionOPD samples actions from the teacher and responses from the current student policy, rewarding student responses according to their semantic agreement with teacher actions. It requires only generation access to a black-box teacher, not teacher token probabilities or tokenizer alignment. The resulting action-level rewards train the student’s own reasoning and action tokens without imposing teacher-written reasoning as a token-level imitation target.
We train Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B students using teachers from two model families on curated tool-use tasks from Agent World Model (AWM) and EnvScaler (Wang et al., 2026b; Song et al., 2026). All four configurations improve average performance across four out-of-distribution agent benchmarks. ActionOPD scales favorably with student size and teacher capability: larger students achieve higher final averages, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash yields higher student averages than Qwen3.7-Flash at both sizes (Figure 1). Comparisons on \(\tau^{2}\)-bench (Barres et al., 2025) show competitive performance against offline distillation and token-level OPD. Ablation studies support the effectiveness of semantic action supervision. We further explore Self-ActionOPD, in which a same-model teacher receives privileged task information. This extension suggests a path toward agent self-improvement through action-level self-distillation.
Contributions.
Our contributions are threefold:
- •
We introduce ActionOPD, an action-level on-policy distillation method that decouples teacher decision supervision from student token realization, enabling distillation from black-box teachers without teacher token probabilities or tokenizer alignment.
- •
We demonstrate ActionOPD’s applicability across teacher families and empirical scaling with student size and teacher capability. All tested configurations improve the four-benchmark average over their corresponding base models by 3.3–6.9 percentage points.
- •
We show that ActionOPD achieves competitive performance against offline distillation and token-level OPD while requiring only generation access to the teacher.
2 ActionOPD
A language-model agent alternates between generating responses and receiving environment observations. At step \(t\), its visible state \(s_{t}\) contains the task, tools, and retained interaction history. Its response \(y_{t}\) realizes a semantic action \(a_{t}=\phi_{s_{t}}(y_{t})\). Here, action level refers to the semantics of a tool call or user-facing message, rather than the token sequence used to express it. The function \(\phi_{s}\) maps responses to semantic actions at state \(s\). We use \(\pi_{\theta}\) for student probabilities over semantic actions, complete responses, and tokens, distinguished by their arguments, and \(\pi_{\mathrm{T}}\) for the teacher. Student and teacher receive the same public context and tools through their native prompts.
To align the student’s action-level behavior with the teacher, we maximize expected teacher-action likelihood at student-encountered states:
|  | \[\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\mathrm{S}},\,a^{\mathrm{T}}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{T}}(\cdot\mid s)}\left[\pi_{\theta}(a^{\mathrm{T}}\mid s)\right].\] |  | (1) |
Denote this expectation by \(J_{\mathrm{ActionOPD}}(\theta)\). Here, \(d_{\mathrm{S}}\) denotes the distribution of student-encountered states collected by the rollout procedure in Section 2.3, held fixed during each policy update.
2.1 From action likelihood to policy gradients
To optimize Equation 1, we express the likelihood of a teacher action as the total probability mass of student responses that realize it:
|  | \[\pi_{\theta}(a^{\mathrm{T}}\mid s)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y\mid s)\,\mathbf{1}\{\phi_{s}(y)=a^{\mathrm{T}}\}.\] |  | (2) |
Here, \(\mathbf{1}\{\phi_{s}(y)=a^{\mathrm{T}}\}\) is the semantic-agreement indicator: it equals one when \(y\) realizes the teacher action and zero otherwise. Agreement is defined by action semantics, not textual identity. Substituting Equation 2 into Equation 1 and exchanging expectations yields an equivalent reward objective:
|  | \(\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{T}}(s,a)\) | \(\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a^{\mathrm{T}}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{T}}(\cdot\mid s)}[\mathbf{1}\{a=a^{\mathrm{T}}\}],\) |  | (3) |
|  | \(\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{ActionOPD}}(\theta)\) | \(\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\mathrm{S}},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)}[r_{\mathrm{T}}(s,\phi_{s}(y))].\) |  |  |
For a fixed state and student action, \(r_{\mathrm{T}}\) averages binary semantic agreement over the teacher’s action distribution. With the state distribution, teacher policy, and semantic mapping held fixed during the update, the score-function identity yields
|  | \[\nabla_{\theta}J_{\mathrm{ActionOPD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s,a^{\mathrm{T}},y}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{\phi_{s}(y)=a^{\mathrm{T}}\}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid s)\right],\] |  | (4) |
where the variables follow the distributions above. This is precisely the REINFORCE policy-gradient form (Williams, 1992), with semantic action agreement as reward (Appendix A.1).
This gradient can be estimated from sampled teacher actions and student responses using student log-probabilities, without teacher token probabilities or tokenizer alignment. Since \(\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid s)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{|y|}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{\ell}\mid s,y_{<\ell})\), where \(\ell\) indexes response tokens, a single semantic-agreement reward weights every token’s contribution to the policy gradient. The supervision is therefore action-level, while optimization covers the student’s own reasoning and action tokens without imposing teacher-written reasoning as a hard target.
2.2 Semantic matching and reward design
We estimate the expected agreement reward \(r_{\mathrm{T}}(s,\phi_{s}(y))\) using sampled teacher responses and a practical semantic matcher. At state \(s\), we request \(K=3\) teacher responses realizing \(a_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}=\phi_{s}(y_{k}^{\mathrm{T}})\) and retain the multiset \(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{T}}(s)=[y^{\mathrm{T}}_{1},\ldots,y^{\mathrm{T}}_{K}]\). The matcher \(m_{s}(y,y_{k}^{\mathrm{T}})\in\{0,1\}\) approximates \(\mathbf{1}\{\phi_{s}(y)=a_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\}\). Invalid outputs never match. Define
|  | \[c(s,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{s}(y,y^{\mathrm{T}}_{k}).\] |  | (5) |
Repeated teacher samples contribute separately to the count. Under independent teacher draws, \(c(s,y)/K\) is an unbiased estimate of the probability that the matcher judges \(y\) to agree with a sampled teacher response. With accurate matching, this probability equals \(r_{\mathrm{T}}(s,\phi_{s}(y))\). Appendix A.3 details sample retention and caching.
The matcher compares action content, not reasoning (Appendix A.3). Tool names and canonicalized arguments are checked programmatically, with a frozen LLM assessing remaining argument differences. For example, get_weather calls with city="Paris" and unit="celsius" are equivalent regardless of argument-key order. For communicative responses, we accept normalized exact matches and otherwise ask the LLM to compare intent and factual content. At the same state, “Basic-economy tickets cannot be changed” and “Changes are not permitted for basic-economy tickets” convey the same restriction. Each candidate is compared with every teacher sample.
Although \(c/K\) estimates agreement, three samples provide only coarse frequency estimates. To limit sensitivity to sampled counts, we assign a unit reward to the first match and a smaller frequency bonus to each additional match. For \(K>1\), we use
|  | \[r_{\lambda}(s,y)=\begin{cases}-1,&y\text{ contains an invalid action},\\ 0,&y\text{ contains a valid action and }c(s,y)=0,\\ 1+\lambda\dfrac{c(s,y)-1}{K-1},&c(s,y)>0.\end{cases}\] |  | (6) |
With \(\lambda=0.5\) and \([A,A,B]\), a response matching \(A\) earns \(1.25\) and one matching \(B\) earns \(1\): the additional \(A\) match contributes \(0.25\), compared with \(1\) for the first match. Invalid outputs incur a separate penalty for protocol violations. The reward in Equation 6 modifies the original likelihood objective. Appendix A.2 derives the objective and its single-sample, binary, and raw-count cases.
2.3 State-group rollout and optimization
State-group rollout.
We use these rewards for both action selection and policy optimization (Figure 3). At state \(s_{g}\), group \(g\) contains \(N=4\) responses indexed by \(i\), sampled as \(y_{g,i}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid s_{g})\) with frozen \(\theta_{\mathrm{old}}\). All candidates are scored against the same teacher multiset, yielding \(r_{g,i}=r_{\lambda}(s_{g},y_{g,i})\). We execute only the parsed action in \(y_{g,i^{\star}}\), where \(i^{\star}\in\argmax_{i}r_{g,i}\), but train on all responses in retained groups. Repeated selection produces one trajectory per task with a teacher-guided visited-state distribution, rather than the ordinary single-sample rollout distribution.
By repeatedly committing high-reward actions, state-group rollout can explore trajectories less likely under ordinary student rollouts, while introducing a degree of off-policy state visitation. Same-state candidate groups also provide local reward comparisons for policy optimization. We use \(N=4\) to balance teacher-guided exploration and deviation from the student’s rollout distribution.
Policy optimization.
Motivated by the REINFORCE-form gradient in Equation 4, we use group-relative advantages and a PPO-style clipped surrogate (Shao et al., 2024; Schulman et al., 2017). Groups without supervision or with identical rewards are excluded from optimization. Equal-reward groups may still advance the environment. Within each retained group, the mean reward provides a local baseline for all \(N\) candidates, including invalid actions with reward \(-1\). We normalize the centered rewards by a shared batch-wide standard deviation:
|  | \[\bar{r}_{g}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}r_{g,j},\qquad\widetilde{A}_{g,i}=r_{g,i}-\bar{r}_{g},\qquad A_{g,i}=\frac{\widetilde{A}_{g,i}}{\sigma+\epsilon}.\] |  | (7) |
Here \(\sigma\) is the population standard deviation of centered advantages over all candidates in the retained groups, and \(\epsilon>0\) stabilizes division. This shared normalization preserves relative advantage magnitudes across different state groups.
Let \(\mathcal{T}_{+}\) index active generated tokens \((g,i,\ell)\) in retained groups. To compare the updated and rollout policies, define the ratio of temperature-scaled token probabilities
|  | \[\rho_{g,i,\ell}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{g,i,\ell}\mid s_{g},y_{g,i,<\ell})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{g,i,\ell}\mid s_{g},y_{g,i,<\ell})}.\] |  | (8) |
Following the token-level expansion in Section 2.1, \(A_{g,i}\) weights every active token in \(y_{g,i}\). PPO clipping discourages excessively large changes in token probabilities. With clipping width \(\eta>0\), we maximize the token-mean surrogate
|  | \(\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\|\mathcal{T}_{+}\|}\sum_{(g,i,\ell)\in\mathcal{T}_{+}}\min\!\bigl\{\rho_{g,i,\ell}(\theta)A_{g,i},\,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{g,i,\ell}(\theta),1-\eta,1+\eta)A_{g,i}\bigr\}.\) |  | (9) |
With the frequency bonus, differing match counts can yield nonzero advantages even when all candidates match a teacher sample. This allows optimization to distinguish among teacher-consistent responses. These updates are not unbiased estimates of the gradient in Equation 4 after group-relative normalization, masking, and clipping. Appendix A.4 details dual clipping and implementation.
3 Experiments
We evaluate ActionOPD’s cross-benchmark generalization, student–teacher scaling, and performance relative to other distillation methods. We then use ablations and case studies to examine its components and decision-making behavior, and explore Self-ActionOPD with privileged teacher context.
3.1 Cross-benchmark transfer setup
Training data.
We trained on Agent World Model (AWM; Wang et al., 2026b) and EnvScaler (Song et al., 2026). AWM provides executable tools and persistent backend state; EnvScaler adds a simulated user to the interaction loop. The curated pool comprised 5,942 AWM tasks from 794 environments and 755 EnvScaler tasks from 49 environments: 6,697 tasks in total. Screening assessed environment integrity and static task feasibility, without requiring successful expert rollouts (Appendix B.1).
Models and training.
We paired Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B students (Yang et al., 2025) with Qwen3.7-Flash (Alibaba Cloud, 2026) and DeepSeek-V4-Flash (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) action teachers in a \(2\times 2\) design. Each configuration used 100 training steps and 64 task starts per step (59 AWM, 5 EnvScaler), with \(N=4\) student candidates, \(K=3\) teacher samples per state, and frequency bonus scale \(\lambda=0.5\). Task starts could revisit tasks in the curated pool. We report the final checkpoints, with Base denoting the corresponding student before ActionOPD training. Appendix B.2 details settings and auxiliary models.
Evaluation.
We evaluated transfer on BFCL Multi-turn (Patil et al., 2025), ToolSandbox’s verified-local-only subset (Lu et al., 2025), AppWorld Normal and Challenge (Trivedi et al., 2024), and \(\tau^{2}\)-bench Airline, Retail, and Telecom (Barres et al., 2025). These tasks were excluded from AWM/EnvScaler training. Students used greedy decoding without teacher-guided action selection. Appendix B.3 details coverage, trials, and interaction protocols.
Metrics.
We report BFCL accuracy, ToolSandbox similarity (not binary success), AppWorld task/scenario goal completion (TGC/SGC), and Tau \(\mathrm{pass}^{1}/\mathrm{pass}^{4}\): single-trial success and four-trial all-success reliability, not best-of-four success (Yao et al., 2025). The average in Table 1 weights the four benchmarks equally, using mean Normal/Challenge TGC for AppWorld and mean domain \(\mathrm{pass}^{1}\) for Tau. Appendix B.3 gives the estimators.

3.2 Main results
Table 1 compares distilled students with base models and directly evaluated teachers.
Cross-benchmark generalization.
After training on AWM and EnvScaler, all four configurations improved the four-benchmark average over their base models by 3.3–6.9 percentage points. Gains extended across BFCL accuracy, ToolSandbox similarity, and AppWorld task completion. These gains on benchmarks excluded from training demonstrate that action-level distillation transfers beyond the training environments, improving average performance across diverse agent tasks.
Scaling with student size.
With either teacher, Qwen3-8B achieved a higher final average than Qwen3-4B under the same task-start and training-step budgets. We used the same candidate count, teacher sample count, and reward formulation for both students. This pattern supports empirical scaling across the two tested sizes, with larger students yielding stronger distilled agents.
Scaling with teacher capability.
DeepSeek-V4-Flash achieved a higher direct-evaluation average than Qwen3.7-Flash and yielded higher student averages at both sizes (Figure 1), supporting empirical scaling with teacher capability. The DeepSeek-to-Qwen results also demonstrate effective cross-family distillation through an action-level interface that requires neither a shared model family nor tokenizer alignment. This allows teacher selection to prioritize decision quality rather than compatibility with the student’s token space.
3.3 Comparison with distillation baselines
We compared ActionOPD, offline distillation (KD), and sampled-token reverse-KL OPD using a Qwen3-4B student, a Qwen3-32B teacher, and the same 178 Tau training tasks. All methods used 24 task starts per step—eight per domain—and were evaluated at step 50. KD collected four independent teacher trajectories per task start and fine-tuned on all reasoning and action/message tokens from successful trajectories. OPD collected four student trajectories per task start and used teacher log-probabilities to supervise sampled tokens. ActionOPD used state-group rollout, sampling four candidates at each visited state while advancing one trajectory per task start. It used \(K=3\) teacher samples per state and \(\lambda=0.5\) (Appendix B.4).

3.4 Ablation studies
Ablations shared the settings in Section 3.3, with \(N=4\) and \(K=3\). Validity Reward replaced the semantic training reward with action validity, while retaining semantic scores for action selection. Binary Reward removed the frequency bonus from Equation 6, using binary agreement for both training and action selection. Random Commit retained ActionOPD’s reward but selected uniformly from all candidates, including invalid or zero-reward actions. Appendix B.4 details the variants and their effects on trainable state groups.
Table 3 reports results on the training set and 100 held-out tasks. ActionOPD outperforms all three variants in average single-trial success on both splits, supporting the combined design. The largest drop occurs with Validity Reward, which assigns identical rewards to all valid candidates and thus provides no learning signal in all-valid groups. Semantic rewards can instead distinguish these candidates by their agreement with teacher actions, highlighting the value of supervising action meaning beyond protocol compliance.

3.6 Self-ActionOPD
We further examine whether a model can provide useful action-level supervision for itself when given privileged task information. Self-ActionOPD shared current Qwen3-4B weights between teacher and student, as in OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026), but independently sampled their responses. Teacher responses were refreshed from the updated shared model at each collection step and held fixed during subsequent policy optimization. The student received only public interaction context, whereas the teacher additionally received action guidance, summaries of private user goals and constraints, and reference actions and target outcomes. Training used action-agreement rewards on student-generated candidates only. Appendix B.5 provides implementation details.
After 50 steps, Self-ActionOPD improved test average \(\mathrm{pass}^{1}/\mathrm{pass}^{4}\) over Base by 4.8/2.5 percentage points (Table 5). Single-trial success also improved across all three domains. Unlike the privileged-teacher reference, the trained student was evaluated using only public context, showing that the student retained benefits from the privileged guidance after training. Together, these results support the feasibility of using a same-model teacher given privileged context for action-level distillation.
4 Related work
Distillation and self-distillation.
Classical distillation transfers predictive distributions (Hinton et al., 2015), while sequence distillation imitates generated outputs (Kim and Rush, 2016). Offline imitation uses token-level losses on fixed demonstrations that may not cover student-induced prefixes. GKD and MiniLLM instead supervise student-generated sequences through token-level divergences (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). These objectives require teacher distributions or sampled-token log-probabilities. Guidance can become unreliable under prefix drift or special-token mismatch (Fu et al., 2026). Cross-tokenizer OPD transfers probabilistic supervision through explicit alignment (Niu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). Privileged self-distillation augments teacher context: On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) minimizes token-level divergence on student rollouts using a shared-weight teacher conditioned on reference solutions (Zhao et al., 2026). SDPO distills feedback-conditioned predictions (Hübotter et al., 2026), while privileged-information distillation studies shared-parameter training, including on-policy reverse KL (Penaloza et al., 2026). Self-ActionOPD instead uses sampled-action agreement.
Black-box and semantic distillation.
GAD jointly trains a discriminator to reward teacher-like responses (Ye et al., 2025), whereas ROPD evaluates responses against rubrics derived from teacher–student contrasts (Fang et al., 2026). On-policy Verbal Distillation (OVD) uses verbal teacher scores to evaluate reasoning quality at step and trajectory levels (Xiong et al., 2026). OmniOPD compares selected student reasoning chunks with teacher continuations sampled at student prefixes (Zhou et al., 2026). MENTOR rewards trajectory-wide tool-set agreement, final correctness, and execution validity (Choi et al., 2025). Action Semantics Learning (ASL) derives a semantic-agreement policy gradient and uses a world model to compare UI transitions induced by generated and reference actions. Its RL variant combines teacher-action agreement with environment rewards (Tang et al., 2026). ActionOPD instead matches immediate tool calls or communicative responses against teacher samples at the same state. Its matcher is instructed to assess agreement, not correctness. ActionOPD requires no reasoning-text matching, complete reference trajectories, or terminal rewards.
Interactive agent learning and process supervision.
DAgger queries experts at learner-visited states (Ross et al., 2011). SCoRe provides local teacher corrections before supervised and short-horizon RL refinement (Lyu et al., 2026). Guided-OPD gradually withdraws teacher turns through a rollout curriculum; ATOD combines OPD and RL with annealed coefficients and turn-level weighting (Li et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026). GiGPO retrospectively groups repeated states across trajectories and derives relative advantages from returns (Feng et al., 2025). ActionOPD scores directly sampled same-state candidates against a shared teacher multiset, without estimating returns.
5 Conclusion and limitations
ActionOPD decouples teacher decision supervision from student token realization through semantic agreement at student-encountered states, requiring only generation access to the teacher. Across tested configurations, it improves average performance on unseen benchmarks, with higher final averages from larger students and stronger teachers. ActionOPD also achieves competitive performance against offline distillation and token-level OPD. Ablations support semantic rewards over validity rewards. Self-ActionOPD improves held-out performance using a same-model teacher with privileged context, highlighting a potential path toward agent self-improvement. Together, these findings support semantic actions as a unit of supervision for agent distillation.
Three limitations warrant attention. First, agreement does not guarantee correctness: teacher errors or suboptimal decisions can become learning targets. Second, LLM matching of communicative responses and tool-argument differences can produce false positives or negatives, misassigning rewards. Third, fixed-size teacher and student sample sets may contain few semantically matching actions when plausible decisions are dispersed, as may occur in long-horizon interactions. Increasing \(K\) and \(N\) could improve coverage at greater generation and matching cost, but the benefit in this regime remains untested.
