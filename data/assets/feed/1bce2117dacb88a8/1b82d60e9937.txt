Practitioner Voice
The Writing Category Nobody has Named Yet
Effective writing from a practitioner is a style distinct from academic writing or thought-leadership content. It relies on authority from experience (not credentials), naming tensions but letting them live, keeping the author in the writing, and the expectation that readers recognize the pattern from their own experience.
13 October 2026
I've been called a Thought Leader.
I don't love the term. But I've used it, because there wasn't another one. When PMI-Agile Alliance introduces me, when conference bios get written, when someone asks what kind of writing I do. Thought leadership is the label that fits the slot. It's not wrong. It's just not quite right.
Three times in my career — at Cutter Consortium, at Thoughtworks, and from Martin Fowler reviewing a manuscript — someone in a position to know told me the same thing: let your voice out. That it kept needing to be said tells you something about the default pressure.
Martin is a co-author of the Agile Manifesto, one of the most respected voices in software development, and someone who has been writing about technical and organizational topics on martinfowler.com for twenty years. His feedback on my Wild West to Agile book was direct: put more of yourself in. I was holding back. I'd written a software history book when I had a memoir trying to get out. Martin saw the gap before I did.
What he was asking for had a character, a way of writing that neither of us named at the time. I've been thinking about what to call it ever since.
A new category of writing
Three ways to disappear
Thought leadership started as a real thing. It meant ideas worth following. Insight that moved a field.
Then it became a content category. Then a marketing function. Then a LinkedIn post with a hook, three lessons, and a call to follow for more. The corruption was gradual and then total. Now “thought leadership content” is a phrase that means almost nothing, or worse, signals the opposite of what it claims. Polish over substance. Brand over argument. Engagement over truth.
The academic route looked like the alternative. If you wanted to be taken seriously, you built the scaffolding. Citations. Caveats. Literature reviews. You buried the lead because that's what rigor looked like. You suppressed your own voice because the author wasn't supposed to show. The problem isn't academic rigor. The problem is when rigor becomes camouflage.
I've done versions of both. I write for PMI where I sit on a thought leadership advisory group. I've written six books. But from the beginning I kept looking for ways to stay in the frame — mountaineering as an analogy for software development in Adaptive Software Development, dialogue to open each chapter of Agile Project Management, interviews with the founders of the major methodologies in Agile Software Development Ecosystems because I wanted their voices, not merely a summary of their ideas. The pressure to conform was real. I was lucky to work with people who either encouraged my instincts or didn't push back on them. That's rarer than it should be.
The problem isn't academic writing or thought leadership individually. At their worst, both do the same thing: they edit consequential human judgment out of the prose. The practitioner disappears behind the rigor or the polish, and what remains is correct but weightless. Academic writing hides behind rigor. Thought leadership hides behind polish. Now a third option has arrived — LLM Voice — that doesn't require hiding at all, because there's no one there. That was a problem worth solving before AI arrived. Now it's urgent.
To come out of hiding, I propose a new writing category: Practitioner Voice.
What Practitioner Voice does
Academic writing builds to the claim. Thought leadership makes the claim and explains it. Practitioner Voice starts with the claim and trusts the reader to close the gap. The author's judgment stays visible throughout. The whole piece organized around what the author has concluded from experience, not what the evidence might tentatively suggest. Practitioner of what? Of anything. The word here means anyone whose authority comes from having done the work and lived with what happened next — mechanical engineer, surgeon, project manager, marketing manager, manufacturing executive. The field varies. The source of authority doesn't.
Underneath all of it sits judgment. You can learn the moves. The judgment has to be earned.
Martin Fowler writes this way. His best pieces don't build to the conclusion, they open with it. The rest is a practitioner showing his reasoning, not constructing a case for a jury. The reader either recognizes the pattern from their own experience or doesn't. He doesn't write for the ones who don't.
When Martin pushed me to put more of myself into Wild West to Agile, he was pushing me toward this writing category. He recognized it before I had a name for it — and he knew the book needed it because the alternative was a history that anyone could have written. Practitioner Voice is the thing only you can write, because it's built from what only you have lived.
What makes it different
Four things separate Practitioner Voice from the alternatives.
Authority from experience, not credentials. Academic authority comes from credentials and methods. Thought leadership authority comes from platform and reputation. Practitioner authority comes from having been in the situation, not as a researcher studying it, but as a person responsible for the outcome. That difference shows in every sentence. You can't fake it and you can't credential your way into it. For consultants the authority takes a slightly different form. We rarely carry direct accountability for outcomes. But if we do our work well, we've been close enough to consequence, across enough contexts and enough years, that the pattern recognition is real. Earned differently. Still earned.
Tension without resolution. Academic writing resolves tension — that's what conclusions are for. Thought leadership resolves it in the three takeaways. Practitioner Voice names the tension and leaves it live. The practitioner knows the tension is real and the reader has to navigate it themselves. Resolving it for them would be lying.
The author stays in. Academic writing never allows this. Thought leadership allows it only as personal brand. In Practitioner Voice the author's judgment is the point. Not the data. Not the framework. The accumulated perspective of someone making consequential decisions for a long time, with something to say about what that's actually like.
The readership contract. Academics write for peer reviewers. Thought leadership writes for eyeballs. Practitioner Voice writes for people who recognize the pattern from their own experience. It's comfortable with limited context — this worked here, under these conditions, judge for yourself whether it fits yours. It doesn't chase universality. It trusts specificity.
Why it matters now
This isn't new behavior. Scroll LinkedIn or Substack and you'll find it: practitioners writing from earned judgment, starting with the claim, keeping the tension live. It's been building for a decade. Nobody called it anything. Without the category, there was no way to teach it, no way to recognize it, no way to say: that's the thing, do more of that.
Now AI produces competent writing at scale — fluent, well-organized, correctly structured, optimized to say nothing wrong. Call it what it is: LLM Voice. The most insidious failure mode of the three because it's indistinguishable from the real thing until you notice nothing is at stake.
AI can mimic the sound of Practitioner Voice. It cannot own the consequences behind it. That's the distinction that holds: not style, not compression, not even the rhythm of earned judgment. Accountability. The willingness to put your name on a call and live with what happens next. That can't be automated.
Practitioner Voice survives the current moment not because it's protected from AI, but because the thing it's made of — accumulated consequence-bearing experience stated plainly — has to be earned. There's no shortcut and no simulation that closes the gap.
I've been doing this in my PMI articles for the last few years. Not loudly. Not by announcing a new approach. Just by not editing myself out, leaving the judgment visible, letting the tension stand, trusting the reader. If something in those pieces felt different to you, that was deliberate. Consider this the explanation.
We've been doing this for years. We just called it something else because we didn't have a better name.
The Craft of Practitioner Voice
Practitioner Voice is not a tone. It is a discipline: start with the claim, stay visible, ground authority in consequence, compress without losing meaning, and leave real tension live. The goal is not to sound more personal. The goal is to make earned judgment useful to readers who have lived the problem.
The category existed before it had a name. Practitioners have been writing this way for years, on Substack, on martinfowler.com, in Cutter journals, in the margins of conference papers, and finding their way to it by instinct or luck. A good editor. An encouraging colleague. Someone who said: let your voice out. Without the category there were no guidelines. Without guidelines practitioners defaulted to the two models they knew: academic scaffolding or thought leadership polish. This piece is the beginning of guidelines that should have existed.
How to use AI without losing your voice
A year ago, I started thinking of it as alternative intelligence rather than artificial intelligence. Intelligence without consciousness: a different kind of mind, not a lesser one. That reframe matters for how you work with it.
How writers use AI, and what they owe their readers about it, is still unsettled. Two practitioners I respect have landed in opposite places.
Mik Kersten writes: “I decided to write every word in this book and did not use AI for any of the ideating, outlining, or writing. The writing process helped me slow down and contemplate, and I hope the process of reading this book will do the same for you.” The writing process itself is the point. AI would have short-circuited it. Mik’s approach would please William Zinsser, who wrote: “Writing is a form of thinking, whatever the subject.” AI makes it possible to produce without learning. That’s the trap.
Jurgen Appelo takes the opposite position. He wrote with a team of one human and multiple AI agents: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and others. Some sections originated with him; others started with the agents and he refined them. His argument: creativity has always been collaborative. AI is the latest in a long lineage of tools that amplify human capacity. What matters is the experience the reader gets, not who or what generated each sentence.
Both positions are principled. I landed somewhere else.
Parkinson’s changed my cognitive processing. Not all at once. A word that used to surface instantly now takes a beat longer. A thread of argument that once held itself in working memory needs an external anchor. Sixty years of consequence-bearing experience didn’t disappear. The pattern recognition I built across careers in software development, management consulting, and organizational leadership stayed. What changed was the biological system I’d always run it through.
Parkinson’s is my version of a universal condition. Every practitioner has something: attention that fragments under pressure, memory that loses threads, processing that slows with fatigue, language that doesn’t always surface on demand. The biological system we run our judgment through is never perfect. AI, designed well, augments what each of us needs. The discipline is the same regardless of what you’re augmenting: keep the judgment constitutive, or the writing stops being yours.
I started working with AI the way most people do: as a tool. Draft this. Summarize that. Speed up what I was already doing. That lasted six months. Then something shifted. I was in the middle of a complex argument, the kind that used to hold itself together through a long writing session, and I noticed the AI wasn’t helping me execute the thinking. I was thinking through it. My judgment, my pattern authority, my accumulated perspective finding its way forward through an architecture that could hold what my working memory no longer reliably sustained. The argument didn’t happen inside me and get recorded. It happened across both minds.
That was the moment the tool became something else.
Andy Clark and David Chalmers called this the extended mind, proposed as philosophy in 1998, arrived at out of necessity in my case in 2024. They offered a thought experiment. Otto has Alzheimer’s and uses a notebook the way others use biological memory. The conventional view says the notebook is a tool. Clark and Chalmers argue it is part of Otto’s cognitive system, coupled in a direct, here-and-now causal loop. Thinking happens across both. Otto directs his notebook. The notebook does not direct Otto. That asymmetry is everything.
The coupling can build the practitioner or quietly replace them. I developed a test and kept returning to it: is this judgment I earned, or fluency I accepted? It doesn’t catch everything. It catches enough.
That distinction shapes every interaction with AI. Treat AI output as first material, not final material. Use it for structure, contrast, and clarity testing. Use it to find the gaps in your own argument. Do not let it finalize voice. A practitioner who defers judgment to AI one interaction at a time loses the capacity to exercise it independently. The skill erodes quietly, below the threshold of awareness, until the moment it’s needed most.
Augmentation builds the practitioners. Automation replaces them.
What the writer brings
In the first part of this essay, I established where pattern authority comes from. The craft question is how it shows up in the prose.
Practitioner authority rests on consequence-bearing experience, not credential. Academic authority comes from method and institutional affiliation. Thought leadership authority comes from platform size and reputation management. Practitioner authority comes from having been responsible for an outcome, not studying it, not advising on it from a safe distance, but owning what happened next. That weight shows in every sentence or it doesn’t show at all. You can’t install it and you can’t fake it.
This holds whether you write about surgery, software, organizational change, or bridge design. The field is incidental. The relationship to consequence is not.
For consultants the authority takes a different form. Few of us carry direct accountability for outcomes. But years of proximity to consequence, watching patterns across organizations, being trusted with decisions you don’t formally own, learning whose experience to trust and why, builds something real. You know what good judgment looks like under pressure because you’ve watched it fail and succeed across dozens of organizations. That’s not the same as owning the outcome. It’s not nothing either. Earned differently. Still earned.
That different foundation creates a different contract with the reader. Academic-essayistic writing builds the scaffolding first: define, contextualize, qualify, then deploy. Practitioner Voice drops a term into a lived context and trusts the reader to close the gap from their own experience. The scaffolding isn’t missing. The reader is the scaffolding.
Show the scar. The practitioner who has only succeeded has limited range. The one who has failed, learned, and kept going has the full picture. Academic writing contains failure in a limitations section, safely quarantined from the argument. Thought leadership avoids failure entirely; it undermines the brand. Practitioner Voice uses failure as a source of credibility. The scar is the credential. Ground your authority in consequence, not in the degrees on the wall.
Staying visible is harder than it sounds. The framework, the data, the existing literature — these earn their place when they serve your judgment. They become camouflage when they replace it. Your judgment is the point. Stay in the frame.
How the form works
One practical technique before you start: write the back cover first. For a book, this forces three questions before a single chapter exists: who is this for, what will they get from it, and why does it matter now. For an article, write the LinkedIn lede first. Same discipline, different form. If you can’t state the claim in three sentences for a practitioner audience, the piece isn’t ready to draft.
Four principles define how Practitioner Voice works on the page:
Start with the claim. Academic writing earns the conclusion through evidence, argument, and qualification. Practitioner Voice opens with the conclusion and “lets the rest of the piece be explanation, not proof. If the reader has lived the pattern they’ll recognize the claim immediately. If they haven’t, no amount of scaffolding will convince them. Trust the reader to close the gap.
Compression as respect. Write as if the reader already knows more than they can easily articulate. Fewer definitions. Shorter transitions. Declarative statements over explanatory ones. Space between ideas. If something feels obvious to an experienced practitioner, don’t apologize for that; let recognition do the work. Completeness is optional. Resonance is not.
Inspiration and information, blended. Practitioners often write as if these are separate: lead with a story, then deliver the lesson; open with a hook, then provide the data. Practitioner Voice doesn’t separate them. The information arrives inside the inspiration. The insight carries the evidence with it. When it works, the reader doesn’t experience a gear shift from engaging to informative. The piece stays in one register throughout because the judgment and the data are inseparable. If you find yourself writing a section that is purely inspirational or purely informational, that’s the signal. Blend it back.
Consider the tension between Mik Kersten and Jurgen Appelo earlier in this piece. One wrote every word himself; the other co-wrote with a team of AI agents. Both positions are principled. Both carry risk. Naming that tension without collapsing it gives the reader something more useful than either answer alone.
Leaving tension live is harder than it sounds. Name it. Illuminate it. Step back. Do not resolve it for the reader; resolving it would be lying. The tension is real and the reader must navigate it themselves. This is the closing constraint that separates Practitioner Voice from every other professional writing form. Academic writing resolves tension in the conclusion. Thought leadership resolves it in the three takeaways. Practitioner Voice leaves it live because that’s where the judgment lives.
Story: the mechanism that makes ideas move
Facts inform. Arguments persuade. Stories move people to act.
Chip and Dan Heath put it plainly: “A credible idea makes people believe. An emotional idea makes people care. The right story makes people act.” That hierarchy matters for practitioners who write. Most professional writing stops at credible. Practitioner Voice reaches for all three.
Consider the Agile Manifesto, one of the stickiest ideas in software history. The value, “working software over comprehensive documentation”, has been cited, debated, and misapplied for more than twenty years. What made it stick? It was simple without being simplistic, a complex idea in direct language that guides action without prescribing it. It was unexpected; until then, methodologies emphasized architecture and design, and code just followed blueprints. It was concrete; anyone in the software business immediately knows what working software means. It carried credibility from recognized practitioners. It carried emotion for developers exhausted by failing traditional methods.
And it had a story. The Snowbird ski resort. Seventeen people in a room. A manifesto written in two days. That origin narrative has been retold thousands of times. The story of how the idea was born is often stickier than the idea itself.
Practitioners underestimate the power of story. We get tangled in technology and methodology, facts and figures, analysis and concepts. Good ideas get neglected because we fail to engage people emotionally as well as intellectually. A story doesn’t decorate an argument. It is the argument’s delivery mechanism, the thing that makes the reader feel tension before they understand it.
Practitioner Voice is built for this. The scar is a story. The moment of recognition is a story. The project that failed, the client who pushed back, the decision that turned out wrong; these are not digressions from the argument. They are the argument, carried in narrative form. Show the scar is a storytelling instruction as much as a credibility instruction.
Stories don’t have to come from the conference room. A mountaineering moment, a physical consequence, a landscape that forced a decision; these carry the same authority as a project war story, sometimes more. The terrain metaphor earns its place in Practitioner Voice because it connects abstract judgment to embodied experience. Readers who have never managed a software project have still stood at the edge of something uncertain and had to decide.
Craft the story before you craft the argument. Know what happened, who was there, what the stakes were, and what changed. Then build the argument around it. The story is not the illustration. It is the foundation.
Structure: braided narrative
Most professional writing defaults to one of two structures: chronological or logical. Chronological moves through time. Logical moves through argument, problem to solution, claim to evidence. Both are serviceable. Neither is the only option.
The braided narrative weaves multiple threads simultaneously, each developing independently while reinforcing the others. A reader follows several strands at once, and the meaning emerges from their intersection rather than from a single linear argument. The structure is common in literary nonfiction and almost unknown in technical and management writing, which is exactly why it’s worth naming here. It is more difficult to execute than either chronological or logical structure. When it works, it is more rewarding than either.
My Wild West to Agile uses five braids across four eras of software development:
- The evolution of methods and methodologies
- Personal experiences and growth across six decades
- The pioneers who drove each era forward
- The explosion of technology innovation
- The progressive management trends that shaped the field
No single braid carries the book. The argument lives in how they weave together.
John Willis’s Rebels of Reason combines braided narrative with chronology: a spine running from Aristotle to ChatGPT with multiple threads running through it, philosophical, technical, and biographical. The chronology orients the reader. The braids create the texture and the surprise.
The braided narrative suits Practitioner Voice because it keeps tension live across multiple dimensions simultaneously. No single thread resolves while the others are still running. The reader holds several things at once, which is closer to how experienced practitioners think than any single-thread argument allows.
When to consider braids: when your subject has more than one dimension that can’t be subordinated to a single argument without losing something essential. When you find yourself writing parallel tracks and forcing them into sequence, that’s the signal. The braids want to run together.
Three levels of editing
The most common editing mistake is fixing the spelling before the argument is worth saving. I once coached a colleague on his first book. He would write a paragraph, then polish it to death before moving to the next. The momentum never built. I kept pushing him to write more and write faster, get the argument down before refining the sentences. It wasn’t his natural style. He resisted, then adapted. The book got finished and published. The polish habit didn’t kill the book. It nearly did.
Mixing levels too early distracts from the work that matters most at each stage. Three levels of editing serve different purposes and need to happen in sequence.
Developmental editing (challenges thinking). It looks at the piece as a whole: purpose, argument, structure, audience, flow, missing ideas, weak logic, and unnecessary material. The goal is to strengthen thinking and architecture before polishing the prose. Claims that may eventually need support should be flagged, but citation mechanics and reference formatting should not derail development before the argument has settled. Key question: does this work as a piece of writing?
Line Editing (improves the writing). It works at paragraph and sentence level to improve clarity, flow, emphasis, voice, rhythm, transitions, word choice, and concision without changing the core argument. Each paragraph should carry a clear idea, develop it coherently, and connect naturally to what comes before and after. Key question: does each paragraph carry one clear idea, and does the language say what I mean in the way I want to say it?
Proofreading (cleans up the writing). It catches grammar, spelling, punctuation, typos, formatting inconsistencies, and other mechanical errors. This is also the right stage for a final check of citations, references, links, and source formatting. Key question: is this clean, correct, and ready to publish?
One rule on citations: identify gaps early, fix mechanics late. The point is not to ignore evidence. It is to keep the reference apparatus from becoming the tail that wags the article. First make sure the argument deserves to survive. Then make the citations impeccable. Citation placement breaks the train of thought at the moment it matters most. Name the source naturally in prose when it’s relevant, put the full reference at the end, and trust the reader to follow up if they want to.
Citations also exist out of respect for other writers. Years ago I was reading an article online. The further I read, the more familiar it felt. I pulled out my Agile Software Development Ecosystems book and found the entire article copied word for word from one of my chapters. No reference. No attribution. That was more than a citation failure. That was plagiarism. Citations exist because ideas belong to people. Put them at the end so they don’t interrupt the reading. But put them in.
Word choice and context calibration
Simple words are not weak words. They are precise words that don’t require the reader to translate. Every technical term should earn its place by doing something a plain word cannot. When a simpler word exists, use it. When the technical term is the right tool, use that. The test is precision, not prestige.
Before you calibrate the vocabulary, identify the reader. Your target audience is narrower than you think. The more precisely you can picture the person reading this, the easier every word choice becomes.
Context calibration is the deeper skill, and it applies to every vocabulary choice a practitioner makes. A cardiologist telling a colleague that a patient presented with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis uses precise shorthand among people trained to receive it. She tells the family the lungs are scarring and breathing will keep getting harder. Same condition, same doctor, same precision, different context. The clinical vocabulary doesn’t disappear. It stays where it belongs. Before reaching for any term, technical or otherwise, the practitioner asks: does this reader share the context that makes this word useful? If yes, use it. If not, translate.
The same discipline applies to consultant vocabulary. “Organizational agility” in a room full of practitioners who live inside that definition is efficient shorthand; everyone knows you mean how fast we can change direction without breaking and saying it the long way wastes their time. The same term dropped into an executive briefing without translation becomes camouflage; it signals tribal membership instead of transferring meaning. The first time you use a term with a mixed audience, define it once in plain language, briefly, then move. After that the shorthand is earned. Context calibration isn’t a prohibition on professional vocabulary. It’s the judgment call that determines when vocabulary serves the reader and when it serves only the writer.
Here is what that looks like in practice. A recent draft contained this sentence: “The contracting model prices the surface and is silent on the substrate, not because the substrate doesn’t matter, but because it was never in the model. It is the same blind spot as your dashboard, in peer-reviewed form.” Correct, precise, and written for an audience that will stop and work through the sentence.
Rewritten for a practitioner: “The contracting model measures what it can see and misses what it can’t. Same blind spot as your dashboard. Just peer-reviewed.” Same idea. One beat of support. Reader closes the gap. That is the difference, and it is not a cosmetic one.
At the sentence level:
- Use active verbs. Reduce “is” and “was”; build sentences around what things do, not what they are.
- Minimize gerunds ending in -ing where a direct verb works better.
- Use “that” sparingly.
- Plan sentence lengths deliberately; short sentences accelerate, long ones slow the reader down.
- A period is a stop sign. A colon announces something ahead. A dash is a tree branch in the road. Use each one knowing what it does to the reader’s pace.
Who you write for
Knowing who you write for changes every sentence. Academics write for peer reviewers. Thought leadership writes for eyeballs. Practitioner Voice writes for people who have lived the pattern and will recognize it when someone states it plainly. You are not writing for everyone. You are writing for the reader who has lived this pattern or will. Close enough to recognize it when they do. Far enough out that your experience arrives before theirs does.
Practitioner Voice is comfortable with limited context. This worked here, under these conditions; judge for yourself whether it fits your situation. That’s a different contract with the reader than academic or thought leadership writing offers. Both push toward universal claims: academic writing for impact factor, thought leadership for audience size. Practitioner Voice trusts specificity. A piece that says “here’s what I saw and what I concluded, in this context,” is more honest and often more useful than one dressed up as universal truth.
A piece is as long as the tension requires and no longer. Short is not a virtue; unnecessary length is a failure.
Most pieces earn a carry phrase: a closing line that sticks. Not a summary. A reorientation. The reader finishes thinking: yes, that’s the tension I’ve been living with. Not every structure calls for it. The inverted pyramid lets the reader exit once they have what they need. But when the form allows it, know what line you’re building toward before you write the opening.
The category now has a name. The Practitioner Voice now has craft guidelines. What it still needs is practitioners willing to write this way, to stay in the frame, show the scar, and trust the reader to close the gap.
That part can’t be outlined.

Thinking in the 21st Century
The section that follows began as a separate paper. It belongs here because it answers the question the AI section raises but doesn’t fully ground: what actually happens when writing becomes thinking across a coupled human-AI system? The argument appears twice: same claim, different register: first in Practitioner Voice, then in Academic-Essayistic. Read the first version, then the second. The analysis that follows names what shifted and why.
Version 1: Practitioner Voice
The machine that answers back can widen the room you think in — or shrink it while the output looks better than ever.
I was in the middle of a complex argument last year, the kind that used to hold itself together across a long writing session, and I noticed something had shifted. The AI wasn’t helping me execute the thinking. I was thinking through it. My judgment, my pattern recognition, my accumulated perspective — finding a way forward through an architecture that could hold what my working memory no longer reliably sustained. The argument didn’t happen inside me and get recorded. It happened across both of us.
That was the moment the tool became something else.
What I was doing had no name. It wasn’t reading — the words weren’t sitting still while I moved through them. It wasn’t writing — I wasn’t filling a blank page with sentences that started in me. I was working on a text with a machine that answered back. I asked it to find the thread I’d lost, to hold the four things I couldn’t hold in my head at once, to show me the shape of what I’d only half-said. It answered. I pushed against the answer. And in the pushing, something came clear that a moment ago wasn’t in my head and wasn’t in the machine either.
The thought happened between us.
We keep reaching for old words. Did the machine write it for me? Did I just read what it produced? Both questions miss the same thing: each assumes the thinking already finished somewhere, and one of us got handed the result. Nothing finished. The thinking got made in the exchange, as it went.
The roles you keep
The clearest way to see this third kind of thinking is to watch the roles: what the machine does, and what that leaves you to do. They come in pairs. The pair is the point.
Start small. You’re mid-thought and you say, without breaking stride, “put that in the notes.” The thought doesn’t pause. When you look up, the record isn’t a transcript of what you said but a version of what you decided as you spoke. The machine closed the old gap between thinking and filing. Your job is no longer to type. It’s to decide what belongs on the record, and in whose voice. Let that decision go and you’ve acquired a scribe you never asked for. Keep it and you’ve kept the part that was always yours.
Then something larger. Work that would have taken a month takes an evening — but the gift isn’t the speed. It’s the moment, partway in, when the shape of the whole thing rises into view: the pattern you could never make out while you were still down inside the work, doing it. The machine carried enough of the doing that you could step back and see. What you do with that freed attention is the whole question. Spend it on more of the same and you’re on a faster treadmill. Spend it on the thing nobody had time to notice, and your thinking reaches further than it could alone.
And then the largest. A document that was unreadable — dense, airless, impossible to hold — gets remade. Not a word of new information. Only the conditions of reading change. You open it and for the first time you can stand inside the thing it describes. Comprehension becomes inhabitation. And inhabiting is an act, not a state: your task is to enter with your judgment still on, to let a view form inside you rather than get carried through. This is augmentation you can’t point to, and the most fragile of the three, because the same smooth surface that lets you enter can carry you straight through, reading everything and judging nothing.
Notice what runs through all three. Each time, the machine takes on what used to be your task — the filing, the logistics, the reading — and each time your task isn’t abolished but lifted. Filing becomes deciding what to file. Doing becomes noticing what the doing hid. Reading becomes entering. The room you think in grows larger.
Drop the higher task and the room doesn’t grow. The machine takes your place inside it, and the work looks the same while you are less. Keep it and the room is genuinely larger, and you are more.
What’s at stake
Every tool that reaches into how we think produces the same argument. Plato feared writing would hollow out memory. McLuhan argued print rearranged the people who lived inside a print-shaped culture. The quarrel is old. The lesson is narrow. A tool doesn’t rewire the brain. It reconstitutes the system that thinks — you, the tool, the page, the people around you — into something differently capable.
The machine that answers back is the newest member of that system. And the most responsive one yet.
One belief quietly ruins it — the one built into most of how these tools get sold — that the machine’s job is simply to do the work. Accept that premise and you keep the output while the roles that were yours fall away. You file nothing, notice nothing, enter nothing. The documents look as good as ever, which is exactly why you never feel the room shrinking around you. Version 2: Academic-Essayistic
by Barton Friedland
Working with a machine that answers is neither reading nor writing but a third kind of thinking — and whether it sharpens your judgment or quietly replaces it depends on what you refuse to hand over.
You are at your desk with a document open, and something is happening that does not have a name yet.
It is not reading. When you read, the words sit still and you move through them. It is not writing, either — you are not filling a blank page with sentences that begin in you. You are doing a third thing, newer than both: working on a text with a machine that answers back. You ask it to find the thread you lost, to hold the four things you cannot hold in your head at once, to show you the shape of what you have only half-said. It answers. You push against the answer. And in the pushing, an entirely new thought space opens — out past where your thinking could reach alone — and in that space something comes clear that a moment ago was not in your head, and was not in the machine either. The thought happens between you.
We have no word for this form of thinking yet, so we reach for the old ones. Is the machine writing it for me? Am I just reading what it produced? Both questions are wrong, and wrong in the same way: each assumes the thinking is already finished somewhere, and one of us is merely handed the result. But nothing is finished. The thinking is being made, in the exchange, as it goes. And what we call it will decide what we let it become.
So let us name it, even without a settled word. This is thinking with a machine that answers — closer to conversation than to reading or writing, except that your partner has read more than you ever could and will remember none of it next month. You bring the goal and the judgment of what matters; it brings speed and reach and an endless patience for the ground you could never cover alone. And the thing that does the thinking is no longer only you. It is you and the machine, arranged a particular way — and the same task, arranged well or badly, can leave you sharper or leave you slack.
This has happened before
None of this is as new as it feels. Every time a tool has reached into how we think, we have had this argument. Plato, in the Phaedrus, feared that writing would hollow out memory and leave only the appearance of wisdom. Marshall McLuhan argued that print did not merely carry new messages; it rearranged the people who lived inside it. The philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers asked where a mind even stops — if a notebook does the reliable work of memory, is it not already part of the remembering? The lesson of that long quarrel is narrower than either the panic or the hype. A tool does not usually rewire the brain. What it does is reconstitute the system that thinks — you, the tool, the page, the people around you — into something differently capable. The machine that answers is the newest member of that system, and the most responsive one yet. That is the deepening: not a new human faculty, but an old arrangement suddenly, startlingly alive.
The roles you keep
The clearest way to see this third kind of thinking is to notice the roles — what the machine is doing, and what that leaves you to do. They come in pairs, and the pair is the point.
Start small. You are mid-thought, and you say, without breaking stride, “put that in the notes.” The thought does not pause. When you look up, the record is not a transcript of what you said but a version of what you decided as you spoke. The machine has closed the old gap between thinking and filing, and your job is no longer to type. It is to decide what belongs on the record, and in whose voice. Let that decision go and you have acquired a scribe you never asked for. Keep it and you have kept the part that was always yours.
Then something larger. Work that would have taken a month takes an evening — but the gift is not the speed. It is the moment, part way in, when the shape of the whole thing rises into view: the pattern you could never make out while you were still down inside the work, doing it. The machine carried enough of the doing that you could step back and see. What you do with that freed attention is the whole question. Spend it on more of the same and you are on a faster treadmill; spend it on the thing no one had time to notice, and your thinking reaches further than it could alone.
And then the largest. A document that was unreadable — dense, airless, impossible to hold in mind — is remade. Not a word of new information; only the conditions of reading are changed. You open it, and for the first time you can stand inside the thing it describes. Comprehension has become inhabitation — and inhabiting is an act, not a state: your task is to enter with your judgment still on, to let a view form inside rather than be carried through. This is augmentation with no instrument you can point to, and the most fragile of the three, because the same smooth surface that lets you enter can also carry you straight through, reading everything and judging nothing.
Notice what runs through all three. Each time, the machine takes on what used to be your task — the filing, the logistics, the reading — and each time your own task is not abolished but lifted to a higher order. Filing becomes deciding what to file. Doing becomes noticing what the doing hid. Reading becomes entering. And each time, the room you think in grows larger: you can hold more at once, reach further, keep more in view. Drop the higher task and the room does not grow — the machine only takes your place inside it, and the work is the same while you are less. Keep it and the room is genuinely larger, and you are more. The pairing means something only when both halves are kept.
What is at stake
So return to the question the old words could not settle. Is the machine thinking for you, or are you only reading what it made? Both miss the same thing: each imagines the thinking finished in one place and handed to the other. In this third kind of thinking, nothing is handed over unless you hand it.
There is one belief that quietly ruins it — the one now built into most of how these tools are sold — that the machine’s job is simply to do the work. Accept it, and you keep the output while the roles that were yours fall away. You file nothing, notice nothing, enter nothing. The documents look as good as ever, which is exactly why you never feel the room you think in shrinking around you. That is the peril of this new space: it can widen until you hold whole problems at once, or narrow until it is only as large as the last thing the machine made — and from the inside, the two can feel the same.
So the choice was never whether to use the machine. It is whether to keep your half of the thinking — to stay the one who decides, who notices, who enters — and so keep the space open instead of letting it close. That is the whole difference between a mind being sharpened and a mind being quietly replaced. And it is, in the end, what thinking in the twenty-first century will have amounted to — not the machines arriving, but what each of us did with our half of the exchange. The thought space you and your AI can open is genuinely new; whether it stays yours is still, for now, up to you.
What changed and why
The two versions make the same argument. Barton’s is a working draft, included here not as a finished piece but because the contrast is already visible. The distance between them is not content — it’s where the authority lives and who the reader is assumed to be.
Jim’s version opens with consequence. The Parkinson’s passage isn’t decoration — it’s the credential. A reader who has worked with AI recognizes the moment immediately. The thinking didn’t happen inside me and get recorded. It happened across both of us. That’s the claim and the proof arriving together, in one sentence, before any framework appears.
Barton’s version opens with a scene. You are at your desk. Literary, cinematic, carefully constructed. It builds authority through the argument’s architecture. The reasoning is visible at every step, and readers who live inside ideas trust that visibility.
Four specific differences worth naming:
The heritage section. Barton gave Plato, McLuhan, and Clark and Chalmers their own section: three paragraphs of intellectual lineage. The ideas earn their place, but in practitioner voice they land in one paragraph, inside the argument, not as a separate tour. The reader doesn’t need the philosophy lecture to feel the point.
Person. Barton stays in second person throughout, essayistic distance. Practitioner voice starts in first person and shifts to second only when the reader needs to feel themselves in the situation. Authority comes from consequence, not from describing the reader’s experience for them.
Compression. Barton’s version runs about 1,400 words. The practitioner rewrite comes in around 950. Same argument, fewer signposts, shorter transitions.
The explaining. Barton’s version signals its own moves: “So let us name it,” “So return to the question.” Practitioner voice trusts the reader to track the argument without being told where it’s going.
On writing together across registers
Jim and Barton arrived at this argument from different positions, and the two versions show it. The instinct when two writers collaborate is to find a blend: soften the consequence, smooth the architecture, meet somewhere in the middle. That instinct is usually wrong. A blend risks losing both registers without gaining anything from either.
The more useful question is division of labor. Barton’s register does something Jim’s doesn’t: it builds the argument so the construction itself carries authority. Readers who live inside ideas trust that register because the reasoning is visible at every step. Jim’s register does something Barton’s doesn’t: it grounds the argument in a person who lived it and trusts the reader to close the gap. These aren’t competing approaches. They’re complementary ones that reach different audiences.
The braided narrative offers a structural solution for pieces where both registers need to coexist. Not alternating paragraphs. That reads like a committee. More like two threads running together, where the intellectual frame and the lived consequence inform each other, and the meaning lives in the intersection. That’s harder to execute. It’s also closer to what two practitioners with different positions actually produce when they think together rather than write in turns.
Which is, in the end, what this part is about.
Author’s Note: On Writing Better
Every book I’ve written has tried something the previous one didn’t.
My first publication was a research paper in Management Information Systems Quarterly in 1978. Dozens of articles followed in IT magazines through the 1980s and 1990s, before the internet changed what publishing meant. Each one was an attempt to say something clearly to practitioners who needed it. The craft was always part of the work, not separate from it.
In Agile Software Development Ecosystems I interviewed the founders of the major methodologies because I wanted readers to know the people, not just the ideas. What did Kent Beck sound like when he talked about Extreme Programming? What drove Alistair Cockburn to Crystal? The interview format was a deliberate choice to make the history human.
Agile Project Management first edition used deliberate dialogue to open each chapter, characters working through problems in real time, ideas emerging through conversation rather than exposition. The second edition replaced the dialogue with client stories, trading the fictional frame for lived experience. Both were attempts to find the right form for the argument.
Wild West to Agile combined software development history, case studies, and something close to memoir, my working life as one of the braids running through the larger story. Martin Fowler pushed me to put more of myself in. He was right.
I’ve attended writing workshops, read dozens of books on writing and storytelling, and kept asking the same question across sixty years: what form does this idea need to reach the reader it’s meant for? This craft guide is the most direct answer I’ve given to that question.
It is not the final one.
