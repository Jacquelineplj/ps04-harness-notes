EGCForest: Structured Agent Memory Organization for Enterprise Group Chats
Abstract
Enterprise group chats record project progress, decisions, task ownership, and other organizational information valuable to enterprise agents. Reliable retrieval is challenging because evidence spans interleaved threads, channels, and time, with meaning and authority conditioned on the speaker. Neither RAG’s independent chunk retrieval nor memory systems’ summary-based consolidation can reliably recover such distributed evidence. We present EGCForest, a novel multi-view hierarchical memory framework for enterprise group chats. EGCForest organizes an immutable message log into three complementary forests: a Channel forest for temporal workflows, an Actor forest for speaker-conditioned context, and an Artifact forest for deliverable lifecycles. Cross-view connections are enabled by a Placement Map and sparse structural bridges. Built on this memory backbone, EGCForest retrieves and assembles evidence by jointly leveraging message-level relevance, hierarchical context from forest summaries, and cross-view links, recovering distributed evidence. Experiments on three enterprise-chat benchmarks show that EGCForest consistently outperforms RAG and memory-system baselines, improving answer accuracy by up to 20.85 points over MemOS while supporting efficient online retrieval. The code is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/EGCForest-MAD12/.
1 Introduction
Enterprise group chats have become the default medium for daily collaboration inside modern organizations Yang et al. (2026). Beyond casual exchanges, they carry a substantial amount of task-related knowledge such as project progress, ownership assignments, and design rationales. Recent progress in agent systems has raised the demand for reliable enterprise knowledge sources Sun et al. (2026), and group-chat logs provide large-scale, fine-grained evidence of organizational activity. Existing research on group chats, in contrast, has largely focused on participating in the conversation Sapkota et al. (2025), for instance predicting the next speaker, recognizing the addressee, or disentangling interleaved threads, rather than on extracting the organizational knowledge that these conversations contain. Turning raw chat logs into an external memory that an agent can query accurately, with traceable evidence back to the original messages, therefore remains an open problem.
Two properties of enterprise group chats make this task particularly challenging. First, reliable memory for enterprise group chats must preserve their multi-level conversational structure. Messages are temporally ordered, but topics interleave within channels, reply chains mix substantive and tangential exchanges, and decision evidence may span multiple threads, channels, and time periods. As shown in Figure 1, answering an artifact-status question requires tracing its lifecycle across two channels and four participants. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems typically treat messages as independent chunks, favoring locally similar matches while missing the full evidence chain Karpukhin et al. (2020). Memory systems instead compress chats into facts or summaries, obscuring or conflating evidence from distinct threads and decisions Chhikara et al. (2025).
Second, capturing speaker-conditioned semantics is challenging yet essential in enterprise group chats. Unlike dyadic conversations, single-user agent interactions, or participant-agnostic documents, group-chat participants’ roles, responsibilities, and task relations shape message interpretation. The same claim may carry different authority across speakers (e.g., a project owner versus an intern), while teams may refer to the same artifact differently. RAG systems typically represent messages as plain text and use speaker identity only as a metadata filter, thereby overlooking speaker-conditioned meaning and evidential relevance Maharana et al. (2024). Memory systems often encode identity as a namespace or auxiliary tag, which can similarly conflate claims from participants with different responsibilities Packer et al. (2023).
We address both challenges with EGCForest, a structured external memory organized as three complementary forests over a shared message log. The Channel forest partitions each group chat into hierarchical temporal intervals, preserving local context and topic evolution. The Actor forest centers each participant, with separate branches for messages authored by that participant and messages that mention them, making identity a first-class semantic axis rather than a passive metadata field. The Artifact forest organizes messages around deliverables and represents their lifecycle across group chats. These forests are explicitly interconnected. When a message appears in multiple forests, a Placement Map links its view-specific placements to a single immutable log entry, enabling direct cross-view correspondence without content duplication. EGCForest further adds sparse Bridges that connect distinct messages through native reply links and shared salient identifiers. Built on this backbone, a four-stage retrieval pipeline combines view-aware leaf recall, summary-guided supplementation, structural completion, and budget-aware assembly.
Contributions. We make the following contributions: (1) A novel memory framework. We propose EGCForest, a structured memory framework featuring three complementary forests to organize scattered knowledge from enterprise group chats while preserving conversational structure, speaker context, and traceability to original messages. (2) Three-view forest architecture. We construct complementary Channel, Actor, and Artifact forests that capture temporal workflow, speaker-conditioned context, and artifact lifecycles, respectively, with a Placement Map and sparse Bridges enabling efficient cross-view navigation. (3) Structure-aware evidence retrieval. We combine view-aware tree routing and evidence expansion through summaries and cross-view links to recover distributed evidence. (4) Empirical validation. Across three benchmarks, EGCForest delivers consistent improvements over strong RAG and memory-system baselines, outperforming MemOS by up to 20.85 points.
2 Preliminaries
2.1 Problem Formulation
We consider an enterprise workspace with a set of channels11 1 We use channel to refer to a persistent, multi-party group chat, following common enterprise chat platform terminology (e.g., Slack, Microsoft Teams). \(\mathcal{C}\) and a set of users \(\mathcal{U}\). A chat history is an ordered, immutable sequence of messages \(\mathcal{M}=\{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots\}\), where each message is a tuple \(m=(t_{m},u_{m},c_{m},r_{m},x_{m})\) with timestamp \(t_{m}\), sender \(u_{m}\in\mathcal{U}\), channel \(c_{m}\in\mathcal{C}\), optional reply target \(r_{m}\in\mathcal{M}\cup\{\emptyset\}\), and raw text \(x_{m}\).
Our goal is to organize \(\mathcal{M}\) into a structured external memory \(\mathcal{K}\) that an agent can query. As memory quality is hard to assess directly, we use question answering as a measurable proxy for it. Concretely, given a natural-language query \(q\) posed by an asking user \(u_{q}\in\mathcal{U}\), the task is to retrieve a compact evidence set \(\mathcal{E}_{q}=\mathcal{R}(q,u_{q};\mathcal{K})\subseteq\mathcal{M}\) via memory \(\mathcal{K}\), and feed \(\mathcal{E}_{q}\) together with \(q\) to an answer agent to produce an answer \(a\).
2.2 Related Work
Agent memory systems maintain persistent memory across sessions with a single user, typically by compressing conversations into facts, summaries, or memory items Xu et al. (2026); Kang et al. (2025); Latimer et al. (2025). MemGPT Packer et al. (2023) treats the context window as limited main memory and swaps information with an external store, whereas Mem0 Chhikara et al. (2025) incrementally adds, merges, or replaces memory entries. Although effective for single-user assistants, these compression-oriented designs can obscure group-chat structure by treating speaker identity as metadata, merging conflicting claims, and limiting access to raw-message evidence.
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) typically organizes text corpora as flat text chunks, hierarchical summaries, or graph-structured representations. BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009) and dense retrieval (Karpukhin et al., 2020) rank individual chunks by lexical or semantic relevance. Hierarchical methods such as RAPTOR Sarthi et al. (2024) recursively cluster and summarize chunks or retrieval across abstraction levels. GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024), LightRAG (Guo et al., 2025), and HippoRAG (Gutierrez et al., 2024) organize entities and relations for retrieval, with GraphRAG additionally summarizing graph communities. These approaches primarily infer structure from text, whereas group chats expose native structural signals, including reply links, participant roles, and cross-channel membership, that are not fully captured by text alone.
Multi-party conversation understanding spans three directions: inferring participant states (e.g., emotion recognition Ghosal et al. (2019)), modeling discourse structure (e.g., conversation disentanglement Kummerfeld et al. (2019)), and summarizing group interactions (e.g., dialogue summarization Gliwa et al. (2019), decision detection Addlesee et al. (2023)). Recent work couples group chats with LLM agents for ongoing participation, for example by jointly generating responses and predicting the next speaker Wang et al. (2025) or orchestrating multi-party tool use Houde et al. (2025). In contrast, we treat completed group chats as a knowledge source and organize them into structured, evidence-grounded memory for downstream agents.
3 Methodology
EGCForest organizes enterprise group chats into three complementary forests over a shared, immutable message log, preserving structural signals lost when chats are compressed into summaries or treated as independent chunks (Figure 2). Offline, it constructs a Channel forest for temporal workflows, an Actor forest for speaker-conditioned context, and an Artifact forest for cross-channel deliverable lifecycles. Online, four stages retrieve message anchors, supplement them through summaries, recover linked evidence, and assemble a token-bounded context for the answer agent.
3.1 Structured Memory Construction
3.1.1 Three-View Forest Architecture
Immutable Message Log.
The three forests share an immutable log of verbatim messages. Each forest leaf references a single log entry rather than copying its content, preserving traceability to the original evidence and avoiding duplication across views. For each message \(m\), the log stores its timestamp \(t_{m}\), sender \(u_{m}\), channel \(c_{m}\), optional reply target \(r_{m}\), and raw text \(x_{m}\). We further derive a mention set \(\mathrm{mentions}(m)\subseteq\mathcal{U}\) and structured identifiers, such as document codes, from \(x_{m}\) using lightweight regular expressions.
Channel forest.
The Channel forest organizes each channel as a temporal hierarchy, making conversational context retrievable at multiple temporal scales. For each channel \(c\in\mathcal{C}\), we construct a balanced \(k\)-ary tree \(T_{c}\) whose leaves are references to the immutable log entries of messages in \(c\), ordered chronologically. Every \(k\) consecutive leaves or child nodes are recursively grouped into a parent until a single root remains. Each internal node summarizes its contiguous time interval, focusing on participants, workflows, decisions, commitments, blockers, and unresolved issues. Unlike semantic clustering hierarchies, this construction preserves temporal contiguity and event order, supporting retrieval of project progress and decision evolution. The running example summarizes the #backend workflow from merge to release, preserving traceability to message-level evidence, including the QA requirement that governs the transition.

Actor forest.
The Actor forest addresses speaker-conditioned semantics by organizing messages around each participant, enabling cross-channel tracing of an employee’s responsibilities, commitments, interactions, and reported context. For each participant \(u\in\mathcal{U}\), we construct an actor tree \(T_{u}\) whose root separates messages authored by \(u\) from messages that explicitly mention \(u\). Within each branch, messages are partitioned by channel, ordered chronologically, and recursively grouped using the same \(k\)-ary construction as the Channel forest. Internal-node summaries emphasize the participant’s speech acts, responsibilities, expertise, ownership, and reporting relations. The running example aggregates Bob’s authored and mentioned messages, forming a participant-centered record of his project involvement, responsibilities, and interaction patterns.

Artifact forest.
The Artifact forest organizes messages around enterprise deliverables, enabling cross-channel tracing of an artifact’s lifecycle, status, and ownership. We identify artifacts offline by clustering messages that discuss the same artifact. We extract artifact-like identifiers, such as document codes, from messages using lightweight rules and cluster the associated messages using farthest-first clustering. Candidate clusters with highly similar centroids are merged automatically, while an LLM judge resolves borderline cases. We retain clusters with sufficient lifecycle transitions. For each retained artifact \(a\), we construct a tree \(T_{a}\) whose leaves reference its member messages pooled across channels and ordered chronologically. Messages are recursively grouped using the same \(k\)-ary construction as the other forests. Internal-node summaries emphasize lifecycle transitions, responsible participants, and cross-channel status changes. 3.1.2 Cross-View Connections
To surface cross-view evidence efficiently at query time, we build cross-view connections through a Placement Map and a sparse set of Bridges.
Placement Map.
Each forest indexes references to messages in the same immutable message log under a different routing rule. Consequently, a message may have placements in multiple trees across views, providing a direct structural connection between them. To make this link efficiently queryable, we precompute for every message \(m\) the placement table \(\mathrm{Pl}(m)=\{(\text{view},\text{tree\_id},\text{parent\_id},\text{leaf\_pos})\}\) indexed by msg_id in a hash map. The Placement Map supports three structural retrieval primitives through direct lookup rather than tree traversal. \(\mathrm{trees\_of}(m)\) returns the trees containing \(m\). \(\mathrm{window}(m,T,w)\) returns the \(w\) messages adjacent to \(m\) in the leaf order of tree \(T\). \(\mathrm{cotenants}(m)\) returns the sibling leaves that share a parent node with \(m\) across its placements. These primitives make cross-view placements and local structural neighborhoods directly addressable, thereby supporting the multi-view joins used later in retrieval.
Bridges.
Whereas the Placement Map connects multiple placements of the same message, bridges connect distinct messages through strong structural or evidential relations. We construct two bridge types from native reply links and the leaf-level identifiers defined in Section 3.1.1. Reply bridges connect a message \(m\) with its explicit reply target \(r_{m}\) when \(r_{m}\neq\emptyset\). Identifier bridges connect messages sharing a structured identifier, such as a document code or ticket ID. To avoid dense all-pairs connectivity, each message links to at most \(b\) same-identifier messages ranked by temporal proximity. These sparse bridges connect local conversational dependencies and cross-channel artifact evidence, supporting the bridge-hop operator in retrieval.
3.2 Forest Retrieval
Given a query \(q\), retrieval produces a compact evidence set \(\mathcal{E}_{q}\) under a fixed token budget through four stages. \(S_{1}\) retrieves primary evidence via view-aware leaf recall, \(S_{2}\) supplements it with summary-guided nodes, \(S_{3}\) completes the chain through bounded cross-view operations, and \(S_{4}\) assembles the final context. Before \(S_{1}\), we parse \(q\) into a lightweight query specification using deterministic rules that require no LLM calls. The specification records the asker and mentioned entities, including participants, channels, and artifact identifiers, to guide subsequent view selection. We detail \(S_{1}\) through \(S_{4}\) below.
3.2.1 \(S_{1}\) View-aware Leaf Retrieval
\(S_{1}\) combines global message seeding with view-aware tree routing. Global retrieval identifies fine-grained message-level anchors, while tree routing lifts these anchors to coarser structural contexts that capture broader workflows, participant histories, and artifact lifecycles. The selected trees define the search space for subsequent summary expansion and cross-view completion.
Candidate seeding.
Given a query \(q\), we retrieve messages using BM25 for lexical matching and dense retrieval for semantic matching. We fuse the resulting rankings through reciprocal rank fusion (RRF), which combines ranks rather than raw scores with incompatible scales:
|  | \[s_{\mathrm{hyb}}(m,q)=\frac{1}{\eta+\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{bm25}}(m)}+\lambda_{d}\cdot\frac{1}{\eta+\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{den}}(m)},\] |  | (1) |
where \(\eta\) is a smoothing constant and \(\lambda_{d}\) weights dense retrieval. The top-\(K\) messages under \(s_{\mathrm{hyb}}(m,q)\) form the anchor pool. Rather than being returned directly, these anchors are projected to their forest placements to identify relevant trees and select leaf evidence within each view.
View-aware routing.
Using the anchor pool, we score each candidate tree with three complementary signals. Leaf2tree projects each anchor to its host trees through \(\mathrm{Pl}(\cdot)\) and aggregates weighted anchor votes. Root matches \(q\) against each tree’s root summary. Collapsed captures locally relevant content that may be obscured by a tree’s coarse root summary. It scans internal-node summaries by cosine similarity and assigns each tree the score of its best-matching summary. We rank each signal within each view rather than globally, and fuse the resulting within-view ranks using RRF. We select top-ranked trees for subsequent stages and retain anchor messages as primary leaf evidence under fixed per-view quotas, preventing larger views from crowding out complementary ones.
3.2.2 \(S_{2}\) Summary-guided Supplementation
\(S_{2}\) supplements the message-level evidence returned by \(S_{1}\) with summary-level retrieval over the trees selected in \(S_{1}\). A message may weakly match \(q\) in isolation even when its surrounding segment is highly relevant. We therefore score internal-node summaries against \(q\), recovering such messages and providing layered context for the answer model. For each internal node \(n\) with summary embedding \(\mathbf{s}_{n}\), we compute \(\cos(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{s}_{n})\). To avoid overlap, we retain only the highest-scoring node on each root-to-leaf path and expand it into a bounded number of descendant leaves. These leaves are appended with their ancestor summary chain as navigation headers. We place \(S_{2}\) leaves after \(S_{1}\) messages rather than merging raw scores, as RRF and cosine similarity are not directly comparable.
3.2.3 \(S_{3}\) Structural Completion
\(S_{3}\) recovers cross-view evidence via bounded Placement Map joins and bridge hops.
Placement Map join.
To avoid unnecessary expansion, \(S_{3}\) invokes a Placement Map join only when the query mentions at least two resolvable entities. It first locates an evidence message \(m_{A}\) for one entity \(A\) from \(S_{1}\), then uses \(\mathrm{Pl}(m_{A})\) to enter a related placement in the selected view. For queries with an explicit temporal cue, candidates are restricted to leaves occurring before or after \(m_{A}\). Candidates are ranked by similarity to the remaining entity \(B\) and \(q\), and the top candidate is retained only if its score exceeds a threshold.
Bridge hop.
\(S_{3}\) expands high-confidence leaves from \(S_{1}\) and \(S_{2}\) through the sparse bridges defined in Section 3.1.2. For each candidate leaf \(m\), we inspect its incident reply and identifier bridges, and add each reached message \(m^{\prime}\) to the candidate pool as a bridge anchor. For each \(m\), \(S_{3}\) follows at most one bridge into a tree outside the evidence pool, choosing the endpoint most relevant to \(q\). We then inspect a bounded window around the selected endpoint in that tree and adds the most query-relevant neighboring leaf. A small hop budget prevents uncontrolled expansion while recovering reply dependencies and cross-channel artifact evidence.
3.2.4 \(S_{4}\) Budget-aware Assembly
\(S_{4}\) merges and deduplicates candidates from \(S_{1}\), \(S_{2}\), and \(S_{3}\). Since scores are not comparable across stages, we preserve within-stage ranks and add candidates in the order \(S_{1}\), \(S_{2}\), and \(S_{3}\). Each stage is subject to a fixed allocation, and selection stops when the token budget is reached. Selected messages are rendered verbatim with lightweight metadata (channel, sender, lifecycle markers). Summary headers from \(S_{2}\) are attributed to their originating nodes and capped at a small fraction of the budget, allowing the answer model to distinguish raw evidence from generated navigation. This design preserves traceability to the original chat log.
4 Experiments
We evaluate EGCForest through four key questions: Q1 (Section 4.2): Does EGCForest outperform RAG and memory-system baselines? Q2 (Section 4.3): Do the Channel, Actor, and Artifact views provide complementary benefits? Q3 (Section 4.4): Does EGCForest retrieve more relevant evidence than RAG baselines, and how does each retrieval stage (\(S_{1}\)–\(S_{4}\)) contribute? Q4 (Section 4.5): How efficient is EGCForest in terms of offline construction cost, storage footprint, and online retrieval latency?
4.1 Experimental Setup
Datasets.
We employ EverMemBench (Hu et al., 2026), GroupMemBench (Yang et al., 2026), and a real-world internal enterprise benchmark Company. Details are in Appendix A.2.
Baselines.
RAG baselines include BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), Dense (Karpukhin et al., 2020), their reciprocal-rank-fusion variant BM25+Dense (Cormack et al., 2009), and GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024). Agent memory-system baselines include MemoBase (Team, 2025), Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025), A-Mem (Xu et al., 2026), and MemOS (Li et al., 2025).
Metrics.
We report Answer Accuracy, measured against gold answers LLMs. For Company, we additionally report Precision@\(k\) and Recall@\(k\) against gold evidence messages.
Implementation.
Following the official protocol, we use GPT-5 as the answer model on GroupMemBench and GPT-4.1-mini on EverMemBench. We also use GPT-4.1-mini on Company. We use the frozen Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B encoder, set the branching factor to \(k=16\), and retrieve under a 3,000-token budget. Details are in Appendix A.3.

Overall.
Table 1 shows that EGCForest achieves the highest average accuracy on all three benchmarks, exceeding the strongest baseline by \(4.77\), \(8.59\), and \(12.61\) percentage points, respectively. These consistent gains demonstrate its robustness across diverse dialogue settings and question types.
Dataset and category analysis.
EGCForest ranks first or second on most subcategories. On EverMemBench, which contains long-horizon, multi-channel project discussions, EGCForest performs especially well on Fine-grained Recall and Memory Awareness, which require linking evidence across channels and time. Profile Understanding remains difficult because Style, Skill, and Role must be inferred from diffuse behavioral patterns rather than explicit event-level evidence. On GroupMemBench, which emphasizes threaded conversations and asker-conditioned queries, EGCForest achieves \(55.10\) on Implicit and \(40.57\) on Ambiguity. Implicit questions require tracing first-person references through the asker’s history, whereas Ambiguity questions require resolving terminology across speakers. These results are consistent with the benefit of the Actor forest for retrieving asker-conditioned context. EGCForest is less dominant on Abstention (\(81.29\)), where recall-oriented retrieval may surface plausible but non-supporting messages. On Company, EGCForest reaches \(61.90\) on Temporal and \(64.06\) on Update, where the Channel forest preserves workflow chronology and the Artifact forest links lifecycle evidence across channels.
Understanding the baseline gap.
Memory systems may merge claims across speakers or threads when consolidating messages into facts, profiles, or summaries, obscuring attribution, updates, and verbatim evidence. On Company, MemOS obtains only 33.33/36.51 on Multi-hop/Temporal, versus 65.38/61.90 for EGCForest. Raw-message RAG preserves message details but ranks messages independently. On Company, BM25+Dense obtains 53.85/44.44 on Multi-hop/ Temporal questions requiring evidence across channels and time. GraphRAG further abstracts messages into entity-relation graphs and community summaries, diluting reply, attribution, and workflow signals.

4.4 Q3: Retrieval Performance
4.4.1 Comparison with Standard Retrieval
EGCForest achieves the strongest overall retrieval performance, attaining the highest Precision@\(k\) at every budget while maintaining competitive Recall@\(k\)33 3 Precision@\(k\) is inherently low: with 3.6 gold messages per question,P@20 is upper-bounded at roughly 18%.. This result is consistent with its multi-view organization and structural retrieval pipeline, which consider temporal, participant, and artifact context in addition to message-level relevance. GraphRAG obtains the highest Recall@20 but at substantially lower precision, suggesting noisier expansion. Lexical and dense baselines improve more slowly as the retrieval budget increases, likely because they rank messages independently and cannot exploit multi-view structural relations.

4.5 Q4: Efficiency
EGCForest incurs moderate offline cost. Its three-forest construction is slower than lexical and dense indexing but faster than GraphRAG, MemoBase, and Mem0, with a 206 MB storage footprint. At query time, EGCForest achieves the fastest retrieval latency at 65 ms per query, enabled by cached node summaries and constant-time Placement Map lookups. Its answer-token usage is comparable to other memory systems, averaging 2,365 input and 1,300 output tokens per query.

5 Conclusion
As an initial exploration of agent memory for enterprise group chats, EGCForest organizes immutable message evidence through complementary Channel, Actor, and Artifact forests. This structured-memory paradigm enables agents to navigate traceable organizational evidence across time, participants, and artifacts. Experiments on three benchmarks demonstrate consistent improvements across diverse question categories.
Limitations
EGCForest is evaluated through question answering on three enterprise-chat benchmarks. This setting enables controlled comparison of memory quality, while leaving its benefits for broader agent workflows, such as task coordination and long-term planning, to be explored. Moreover, EGCForest’s offline construction time remains manageable at the evaluated scale, making periodic memory updates through full reconstruction feasible. For larger or more frequently updated workspaces, incremental forest construction and selective updates to summaries and cross-view connections are promising directions for balancing memory freshness with maintenance cost. Finally, our current formulation focuses on text messages and their associated metadata. Images, document attachments, and voice messages can contain relevant organizational knowledge beyond the message text. Future work could extend EGCForest to integrate these sources into a unified multimodal memory while preserving traceability to the original evidence.
