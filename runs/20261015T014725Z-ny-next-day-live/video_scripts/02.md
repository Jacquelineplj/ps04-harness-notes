# Which numbers survive the method check

**Runtime target:** about 4 minutes 15 seconds (roughly 610 narration words)

**Visual:** Title card on dark background, then a split panel: left side labelled "SCORE: PASS", right side labelled "STATE: UNCHANGED".
**On-screen text:** The score said pass. The trace disagreed.
**Narration:** Here is the argument in one breath: the strongest measured claims this month are the ones whose authors drew the boundary around their own result before anyone else could, and the weakest are not vendor announcements but widely-shared numbers that moved two variables at once. Underneath all of it sits one methodological finding that research and practitioner work reached independently — if you grade an agent on its trajectory or its final response instead of the state it leaves behind, you will systematically overstate success. [02-c8]

---

**2.**
**Visual:** Quote card — "Hugging Face, 3 October 2026 — The Agent Said It Was Done. The Database Disagreed." Below it, a large stat block: 121,680 trials / 79,853 failures / 67.24%.
**On-screen text:** 67.24% of failures terminated cleanly
**Narration:** Start with ThinkingBox, from Microsoft's Copilot Studio team, published on Hugging Face on the third of October. They report that across 121,680 valid trials, 79,853 attempts failed executable state checks — and 67.24 percent of those failures still terminated cleanly, invoked a state-changing tool, and reported no final tool error. [02-c1] Read that slowly. If your eval grades trajectory shape or the final response, two-thirds of your real failures are being scored as successes.

**3.**
**Visual:** Quote card — "Arize, 12 October 2026 — How to test agent tool descriptions with Harbor and Phoenix." Simple before/after bar pair: skill loads 51 → 0; cost $11.85 → $8.34.
**On-screen text:** One tool description. 51 loads to zero.
**Narration:** Arize's Harbor and Phoenix post from the twelfth is the practitioner version of the same thing. They report that reward was one across the board — every run returned the right answer — and yet Codex loaded an unnecessary skill on 51 of 54 attempts. Changing a single tool description cut those loads from 51 to zero and the benchmark run cost from eleven dollars eighty-five to eight thirty-four, with correctness unchanged. [02-c2] Two publishers report a similar limitation. That convergence is suggestive, but both have commercial interests.

**4.**
**Visual:** Side-by-side prompt cards. Left: "RUDE — Do not waste my time or give any extra text." Right: "POLITE — (no length instruction)." The brevity clause highlighted in red on the left only.
**On-screen text:** Two variables, one headline
**Narration:** Now the tension worth reporting, which is between a published result and its own re-run. The "Mind Your Tone" finding — that rude prompts improve accuracy — circulated widely. Arize's Jim Bennett rebuilt the experiment and published on the thirteenth. He reports that the paper's rude prompts also carried brevity instructions, while the polite prompts said nothing about length. Deleting only the brevity words cut Opus's apparent rudeness saving from 55 percent down to 15. [02-c3] We're not manufacturing a fight here — the re-run is explicitly a re-run, and the vendor is the one who did it.

**5.**
**Visual:** Bar chart described in words: three bars, mini-SWE-agent 49.3%, Terminus 2 57.3%, LAGO 60.3%, with a translucent ±8 point error band overlaid across all three.
**On-screen text:** 60.3 vs 57.3 — inside the noise band
**Narration:** LAGO, posted to arXiv on the fourteenth, is the hygiene model. Rather than importing published numbers, the authors re-ran mini-SWE-agent and Terminus 2 end to end on the same policy, temperature, task IDs and evaluator — reporting 60.3 percent for LAGO against 57.3 for Terminus 2 and 49.3 for mini-SWE-agent on a fixed 150-task subset. [02-c4] Then they did the second thing. They stated that at n equals 150, a resolve-rate difference carries roughly plus or minus eight percentage points of binomial uncertainty. [02-c5] So the three-point gap over Terminus 2 sits inside that band. The gap over mini-SWE-agent does not. Quote both facts in the same sentence.

**6.**
**Visual:** Two stacked source strips. Top: "RATED — arXiv, 9 Oct 2026" with text "+52.2% MRR vs ToolLLM — still below Ada-002 and SPLADE-v3." Bottom: "X-AgentRed — arXiv, 9 Oct 2026" with "1.77× harmful path rate · 2.99× tool coverage."
**On-screen text:** Offline accuracy ≠ runtime reliability
**Narration:** Two more, briefly. RATED reports that its full model improves mean reciprocal rank by 52.2 percent over the ToolLLM retriever on real queries while still ranking below two simpler encoders on that same metric — and the authors conclude that high offline ranking accuracy does not guarantee robust tool selection when tools fail at runtime. [02-c6] And X-AgentRed reports finding harmful cross-service tool paths at 1.77 times the rate of the STAC baseline, while covering 2.99 times as many distinct tools, across four frontier agentic models. [02-c9] Note the shape there — a hit-rate claim and a coverage claim reported together, because either one alone is gameable.

[02-c7] We think a vendor declining to claim a number it easily could have claimed tells you something about the rest of the deck.

**Narration:** The honest limitation: ThinkingBox comes from a team with a commercial stake in reliability tooling and every task is a synthetic reconstruction, not real traffic. Both Arize pieces are vendor-authored by a company selling tracing. RATED, LAGO and X-AgentRed are preprints with no independent replication. We have no third-party check on any vendor-reported number in this edition. So: add a state check to one eval suite this month. Record cost and tool count next to reward. Change exactly one variable per experiment. And quote the uncertainty band whenever you quote the score. Full article and every source are linked in the description.
