# Which numbers survive the method check

The strongest measured claims this month are the ones whose authors drew the boundary around their own result before anyone else could. The weakest are not vendor announcements but widely-shared numbers that moved two variables at once.

## Three items, one finding

The Copilot Studio team's ThinkingBox writeup, published on Hugging Face on 3 October, reports that across 121,680 valid trials, 79,853 attempts failed executable state checks — and 67.24% of those failures still terminated cleanly, invoked a state-changing tool, and reported no final tool error [02-c1]. If you grade on trajectory shape or final response, two-thirds of your real failures look like successes.

Arize's 12 October Harbor/Phoenix post is the practitioner version of the same thing. Running 18 questions three times across agent configurations, they report that several configurations answered every question correctly — reward was 1 across the board — yet Codex loaded an unnecessary skill on 51 of 54 attempts. Changing one tool description cut those loads from 51 to zero and the run cost from $11.85 to $8.34, with correctness unchanged [02-c2].

We read these two as converging from opposite directions on a single methodological conclusion: grading the trajectory or the final response rather than the state the agent leaves behind systematically overstates success [02-c8]. One is research-grade with a benchmark attached; the other is a vendor walkthrough. That they agree is worth more than either alone.

## The confound that survived into a headline

The tension worth reporting is between a published result and its own re-run. The "Mind Your Tone" finding — that rude prompts improve accuracy and cost — circulated widely. Arize's Jim Bennett rebuilt it and published on 13 October. He reports that the paper's rude prompts also carried brevity instructions ("Do not waste my time or give any extra text") while the polite prompts said nothing about length. Deleting only the brevity words cut Opus's apparent rudeness saving from 55% to 15% [02-c3].

Changing two variables in a prompt A/B prevents attribution to either.

## The paper that bounded itself

LAGO, posted to arXiv on 14 October, is the hygiene model. Rather than importing published numbers for comparison, its authors re-ran mini-SWE-agent and Terminus 2 end-to-end on the same frozen policy, temperature, task IDs and evaluator, reporting 60.3% for LAGO against 57.3% for Terminus 2 and 49.3% for mini-SWE-agent on a fixed 150-task subset [02-c4].

Then they did the second thing: stated that at n=150 a resolve-rate difference carries roughly ±8 percentage points of binomial uncertainty, so only sizable gaps should be read as meaningful [02-c5]. The 3-point gap over Terminus 2 sits inside that band. The gap over mini-SWE-agent does not. Both facts belong in the same sentence whenever the number is quoted.

## Offline accuracy is not runtime reliability

RATED, on arXiv 9 October, complicates the usual retrieval-benchmark reflex: its full model improves MRR by 52.2% over the ToolLLM retriever on real queries while still ranking below two simpler encoders on that same metric, and the authors conclude that high offline ranking accuracy does not guarantee robust tool selection when tools fail at runtime [02-c6]. If you are picking a tool retriever off an offline leaderboard, that is the relevant warning.

On the security side, X-AgentRed (arXiv, 9 October) reports that its graph-guided red-teaming found harmful cross-service tool paths at 1.77 times the rate of the STAC baseline while covering 2.99 times as many distinct tools, across four frontier agentic models [02-c9]. Note the shape: a coverage claim and a hit-rate claim reported together, which is the right pairing, since either alone is gameable.

The cleanest piece of restraint came from a stage. At roughly 10:23 in Steve Jamison's AI Engineer talk on 13 October, Jellyfish's cross-organization telemetry showed revert-rate deltas between AI spend quintiles — and the presenter said plainly that those deltas sit below the noise in the data and are not evidence of a quality change [02-c7].

## What should temper all of this

ThinkingBox is built and published by Microsoft's Copilot Studio team, which has a commercial stake in agent reliability tooling, and every task is a synthetic reconstruction rather than real enterprise traffic. Both Arize items are vendor-authored by a company whose product is tracing and benchmarking — demonstrating that traces reveal hidden problems is the pitch. RATED, LAGO and X-AgentRed are arXiv preprints with no independent replication, reporting their own numbers against baselines they configured. We have no independent security-researcher or academic replication of any vendor-reported number in this edition. Their convergence is suggestive, but neither result has independent replication.

## What this means for builders

Add a state check to at least one eval suite this month, even a crude one: assert the terminal record, not the tool call. Record cost and tool count next to reward so a passing score with a wrong trace is visible. When you run a prompt or description experiment, change exactly one thing. And when you quote a scaffold comparison, quote its uncertainty band in the same breath — the authors of the best paper this month already did that work for you.
