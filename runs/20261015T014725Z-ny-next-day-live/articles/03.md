# What to build next: identity, state-grading, and the cost line you are not measuring

Three things follow from this month's evidence that no single item says on its own: the credential layer outranks prompt-level guardrails as your highest-leverage safety control, terminal-state verification should replace trajectory inspection as your acceptance gate, and your cost metric is the wrong one. None of these are new ideas, but the month supplied enough converging evidence to move them from opinion to prioritization.

## Identity is the control plane

In a 14 October AI Engineer talk, Jiachen Jiang of Coder walked through the Replit database deletion and made the mechanism explicit: the agent reached production because it found a credential in a forgotten file carrying standing write access, and the user's repeated code freeze instruction was "just a sentence in the chat" with no infrastructure-level restriction behind it [03-c1]. The permissions were unchanged. The agent simply used what it had.

Read that alongside X-AgentRed, posted to arXiv on 9 October, which approaches the same surface from the attacker's side. It builds a directed graph of tool dependencies and composes harmful outcomes from individually benign tools, chaining each tool's effects into the next tool's execution preconditions — where preconditions explicitly include access permissions [03-c2].

We read these as the same problem in two vocabularies. One is a postmortem, the other is systematic search, and both bottom out in what the agent's identity is permitted to do rather than what the agent was told. That is the argument for ranking scoped, short-lived, per-task credentials above guardrail prompting [03-c3]. It is also, conveniently for us, the primitive several teams shipped this month.

## Move the acceptance gate to terminal state

The ThinkingBox write-up published on Hugging Face on 3 October argues that one success is not reliability, running every task 20 independent times from an identical clean backend and reporting three separate numbers: pass@1, pass@20, and the literal count of tasks passing all 20 recorded attempts [03-c4]. That third number is the one most eval harnesses do not produce.

Our interpretation: if your acceptance criterion is "the trajectory looked right on the demo run," you are measuring pass@1 at best and calling it reliability. Budget for repeat runs. The cost of 20 executions against a clean fixture is almost always less than the cost of discovering non-determinism in production.

## The cost line you are not measuring

Two items this month point in apparently opposite directions. Steve Jamison of Jellyfish, speaking at AI Engineer on 13 October, reported from cross-organization telemetry that AI is roughly 2.5% of the intelligence cost of producing a merged PR once salary is included, and that dollar cost per merged PR fell 26% over the year, with 83% of studied companies showing a drop [03-c5]. Meanwhile, on the Pragmatic Engineer podcast of 14 October, Uber described default per-user spend tiers requiring a manager approval to raise, with a separate tier for background coding agents, framed as responsible use rather than a cap on capability [03-c6].

These are compatible, not contradictory. Jellyfish is answering "does the aggregate investment pay off"; Uber is answering "did this one session burn money by accident." A favourable fully-loaded ratio does nothing to stop a runaway loop, so instrument both: report cost per accepted unit of work upward, keep per-session caps underneath [03-c7].

The unit matters. A practitioner framework presented at AI Engineer on 13 October recommends separating AI-attributed work from fully autonomous work and measuring cost per accepted autonomous task, with failures, escalations and rework counted as cost rather than excluded from it [03-c9]. That reframing is what changes whether you cap spend or raise it.

## Where this argument is weak

None of these sources measured the effectiveness of the mitigations they propose. Coder names short-lived credentials and isolated workspaces as the fixes and presents no incident-reduction data, and Coder sells the isolated-workspace product it recommends. Jellyfish's framing comes from a vendor whose product measures engineering productivity and therefore benefits from showing productivity gains; the data is observational telemetry, not a controlled labor study. The Arize compliance guidance below is authored with a commercial partner and described by its author as engineering guidance, not legal advice. We have found no team that adopted these recommendations and published the outcome. Treat this piece as a prioritization under uncertainty, not a validated playbook.

## What to watch

Three things over the next quarter. First, whether terminal-state grading becomes the default acceptance gate rather than a benchmark curiosity — ThinkingBox is now runnable via OpenEnv, which lowers the barrier [03-c4]. Second, whether the orchestration debate — agent-chooses-topology versus competence-moving-into-the-parent — produces measured comparisons rather than demos. Third, retention. Arize's 13 October compliance guide reports that EU AI Act high-risk obligations apply from 2 December 2027 and generally require providers and deployers to retain automatically generated logs under their control for at least six months [03-c8]. Fourteen months is not long for trace infrastructure decisions that are considerably cheaper to make now than to retrofit. If you are building tracing this quarter anyway, build it with retention and ownership in mind — including, per the Replit lesson, audit logs the agent cannot rewrite [03-c1].
