# Which Harness Claims Survive Contact With Method

This month's strongest sources describe methods, matched budgets and uncertainty. Their findings differ with the tasks; the useful contribution is often a negative result and the condition that explains it.

## The month's best-evidenced claim refutes its own authors

The clearest piece of method this month is a 9 October arXiv preprint on sandbox snapshots for coding agents, co-authored from Daytona. The authors state a hypothesis — branching from promising snapshots should beat independent retry under a fixed token budget — and then report that it failed. Under a matched one-million-token budget on a pre-registered harder Terminal-Bench subset, independent retry solved 17 of 25 task-seed pairs while snapshot branching solved 6 of 25 [02-c1].

What makes the paper useful is that it does not stop at the refutation. It isolates the condition under which full snapshots do win: on a custom task whose progress lived in a SQLite database outside git, full snapshot restore solved 4 of 5 attempts while source-only restoration solved 0 of 5 [02-c2]. That is the shape of a conditional result — hypothesis, matched budget, refutation, boundary.

## Evaluation rigs that cannot see what they claim to measure

A 3 October Hugging Face post on the ThinkingBox benchmark makes a point about repeated sampling that is easy to miss when reading leaderboards. It reports that Claude Opus 5.5 scored higher than Claude Opus 5 on the single-attempt average yet passed exactly the same number of tasks on all 20 attempts — 241 — so the headline gain bought no additional dependability [02-c4]. We read this as a statement about measurement, not model quality: a pass@1 column answers a question most production teams are not asking.

The same reading applies to Jellyfish's cost-per-PR analysis, presented by Steve Jamison at the AI Engineer event on 13 October. Discussing revert rates as a proxy for work quality at around 10:23 in the talk, he explicitly marks the deltas as slightly below the noise in the data and says it is not evidence of an improvement [02-c8]. That restraint matters.

## Vendor claims, labelled and unlabelled

Microsoft's 16 September post comparing distributed skills over MCP against specialist agents is unusually careful for a vendor piece. It reports that the skills path consumed about 22% more tokens across three runs despite making fewer model calls, and the authors themselves label the comparison as not a controlled performance or quality study [02-c5]. The honesty is real; it is still three runs, and three runs do not establish an architectural property.

Two items remain unverified by construction. The X-AgentRed red-teaming preprint, also dated 9 October, reports 1.77x the harmful path rate and 2.99x the tool coverage of the STAC baseline across four frontier agents — but states that each configuration was evaluated in a single run within a fixed simulated ecosystem [02-c6]. And on the Dwarkesh podcast released 17 September, OpenAI's Noam Brown, discussing the reported 10,000-agent Navier-Stokes run, says around 5:04 that the experiments are simply too expensive to ablate at that scale and that the team does not yet know how long a single agent would take [02-c7]. We take that as a model of how to state an unablated result: the number is real, the causal claim is not available.

## The conflicts dissolve into task dependence

Snapshot restoration and harness scaffolding both look, from a distance, like general wins. Both turn out to be conditional, and the sources themselves say so. Thoughtworks' 13 October writeup of a harness experiment reports its own refuted expectation up front: a behavioural harness is not an automatic win for every model on every task, and for one model running the harness continuously added overhead without a detected acceptance benefit. The snapshot paper lands in the same place from a different direction — for file-centric tasks, fresh retries suffice; when progress lives outside the repository, full snapshots become compelling [02-c9]. Reading these as a disagreement is a mistake. They are two instances of the same conditional, and naming the condition is the analytical contribution.

## What we could not check

Several of the better-methodology items are preprints without independent replication, and two carry commercial proximity worth stating plainly: the snapshot paper is co-authored from Daytona, whose sandbox product it evaluates, and this edition's benchmarking walkthrough from Arize promotes Arize's own tooling. ThinkingBox's cost figures are described as estimated list-price indices rather than invoices, and its benchmark tasks are synthetic reconstructions. No security researcher independently evaluated X-AgentRed's harmful-path rates. The largest gap is the one we cannot close from the desk: none of these studies were replicated by a party with no stake in the outcome. We found no counter-result in the retained sample; that absence is not corroboration.

## What this means for builders

Stop asking whether snapshots, scaffolding or skills-over-MCP help, and start asking under which condition. Before adopting snapshot restoration, check whether your tasks' progress actually escapes the repository; before adding a continuous behavioural harness, check whether your model's unaided attempts are already consistent, and if they are, run the sensors once as a final gate instead. And when you benchmark your own agents, report the all-attempts column alongside pass@1 — several of this month's most useful findings only became visible because someone bothered to.
