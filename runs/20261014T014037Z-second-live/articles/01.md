# The Harness Moved Up a Layer: What Changed in Agent Infrastructure This Month

The harness is absorbing jobs that used to belong to the model or to the human operator: picking tools by how reliably they execute, writing its own orchestration program, and grading itself on backend state instead of tool calls. Most of this month's items are announcements, but two carry real measurements — and those two are the ones worth reading closely.

## The one controlled experiment

Naresh Jain's piece on martinfowler.com, dated 13 October, is the cleanest result of the month because it holds the task fixed and varies only the harness. He ran the same API-service build ten times per condition and reports that a cost-optimized model, GPT-6 Luna, went from 3 of 10 accepted runs unharnessed to 10 of 10 with a behavioral harness, while the frontier model GPT-6 Sol passed all ten either way [01-c1].

Our interpretation: harness benefit depends on the model. Testing only your strongest model may hide a benefit available to a cheaper one.

## Graders that look at the wrong thing

The second measured item is ThinkingBox, published on Hugging Face on 3 October, which grades agents on terminal database state and side effects rather than on trajectories. Across a common-set ablation of 12 models, the authors report 79,853 failed attempts, of which 67.24% nonetheless terminated cleanly, invoked a state-changing tool, and reported no final tool error [01-c2]. A tool-call-based grader would have scored two thirds of real failures as fine.

Arize's Harbor/Phoenix walkthrough, posted 12 October, hits the same blind spot from the opposite end — small scale, production tooling. Arize reports that every configuration passed the correctness benchmark, but inspecting traces revealed Codex loading an unnecessary skill repeatedly; changing a single tool description cut those loads from 51 to zero and the run cost from $11.85 to $8.34, with correctness unchanged [01-c3]. The score was never going to find that. The trace did.

## Retrieval that knows what's broken

The RATED paper, posted to arXiv on 9 October, applies the same logic one layer down. Tool retrieval has historically ranked on semantic relevance; RATED adds a contextual bandit that re-ranks on live execution feedback. The authors report that under a simulated 30% API failure rate, fault selection rate dropped from 86.2% to 2.9%, with under 1 ms of added latency [01-c4]. If that holds outside simulation, it is cheap enough to be uncontroversial.

## Orchestration the model writes

On the vendor side, Latent Space reported on 12 October that Anthropic's new "dynamic workflows" lets Claude write its own orchestration program, replacing the earlier requirement that developers configure a coordinator and a roster of subagents [01-c5]. That is a claim about a product surface, not a measured reliability result, and our retained evidence contains no independent measurement of it.

Related context on scale: Simon Willison, in a 13 October link post citing Anthropic's own engineering writeup, reports that merged code changes at Anthropic rose from roughly 2,500 per day in May 2026 to over 30,000 per day in September, prompting a custom source-control system sized for about 25 million checkouts per day [01-c6]. Treat that as an existence proof of what sustained agent throughput does to infrastructure, not as a number you can generalize from.

And on the Latent Space podcast from 29 September, Anthropic's Thariq Shihipar said at 46:22 that the core Claude Code harness has to be "quite complex and very secure" — sandbox, permission approvals, computer use, MCPs, web access — even as the interaction layer above it changes freely [01-c7]. That matches what the rest of the month shows: the interface churns, the substrate accumulates.

## The through-line, and what it isn't

Our interpretation: all of these are the same move. RATED stops trusting semantic relevance and watches execution; ThinkingBox stops trusting tool calls and watches state; Arize stops trusting the score and watches the trace [01-c8]. The recurring failure is a harness deciding on a proxy that doesn't carry the information the decision needs.

## Where this evidence is thin

Narrower than it looks. Jain's experiment is one greenfield Java service with an unusually detailed brief, ten runs per condition, at low reasoning effort — he says so himself [01-c1]. RATED's reliability gains come from injected failures in simulation, with live validation listed as future work [01-c4]. The Anthropic orchestration item is conference reporting of a vendor talk, with no independent measurement attached [01-c5]. Absent entirely: open-source maintainers of competing orchestration stacks, security researchers probing these new orchestration surfaces, and — most relevant to readers — any measurement of harness effects on legacy codebases rather than greenfield ones.

## What this means for builders

Treat harness design as the live variable, and test it against the model you actually ship, not your best one [01-c1]. If your eval grades tool calls or final responses, assume it is blind to a large share of real failures and add a state-level check before you trust a green run [01-c2]. Keep traces next to scores in your eval output — the cheapest win this month came from reading tool counts, not pass rates [01-c3]. Everything else on this list is a claim awaiting method.
