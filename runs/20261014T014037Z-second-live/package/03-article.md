# What To Change in Your Harness, and What To Watch

This month's practical question is where to spend review effort: on terminal state, repeatability, and feedback cadence matched to the model. Several sources also point to an organisational constraint: someone must own the agent's decisions.

## Change one: grade the terminal state, not the summary

The ThinkingBox authors, in their 3 October Hugging Face post, give a short list that is unusually easy to act on: check the terminal state before you commit rather than trusting the model's account of what it did, classify tool and system errors so retries target the recoverable ones, cut the tool surface to what the workflow actually needs, and require human approval on changes you cannot cheaply reverse [1]. The part worth respecting is the caveat attached to it — they say plainly that they have not measured the lift from any of these [1]. Treat these as hypotheses to test in your own environment.

A related failure mode showed up on the MLOps Community podcast on 28 September, where an AWS practitioner described shipping an MCP documentation change that passed their eval matrix and then, in production, caused agents to flag the Markdown docs as an attempted malicious injection — forcing a rollback they summarised as "we didn't know our matrix wasn't big enough" [7]. We read that as the strongest available argument that eval matrices for tool and documentation changes are routinely too narrow, and that documentation is part of the harness surface you have to test.

## Change two: pick cadence from the model, not the principle

Thoughtworks published Jain's harness-cost write-up on 13 October, and the conclusion is narrower than the title implies. What changes with model capability is not whether independent sensors are needed but when they run: continuous feedback during development for models whose unaided attempts produce inconsistent outcomes, and a single final acceptance gate for frontier models that pass the brief in one shot [2]. If you are running a continuous harness against a model that already solves your tasks unaided, you are paying development overhead for a verification you could get once at the end.

## Change three: stop treating tokens as the thing to cap

Steve Jamison of Jellyfish, speaking at AI Engineer on 13 October, reported that AI spend is roughly 2.5% of the combined human-plus-AI intelligence cost of producing a merged PR, and that dollar cost per merged PR fell 26% over a year at 83% of the companies studied [3]. If that holds for your org, a tight token budget is more likely capping throughput gains than capping waste. Note the incentive: Jellyfish sells engineering analytics, and merged PRs are a throughput proxy the talk itself concedes may reflect larger PRs rather than more work.

## The ownership constraint, and why it is a harness problem

Michele Grant, speaking at AI Engineer on 13 October, laid out a four-stage autonomy ladder — shadow, supervised draft, exception ensemble, exceptions-only — and warned that once humans review only exceptions, the biggest vulnerability becomes what the agent failed to flag, so a falling override rate may mean review depth has slipped rather than the agent improving [4]. Paden Gayle, from a bank-deployment angle at the same event, made what we read as the same structural point from the other end: preserve the input prompts, model version, configuration, output and reviewer action, because calling the model again later may produce a different answer — which makes the preserved record, not the model, the system of record for a past decision [5]. Arize's EU AI Act engineering guide, published 13 October, arrives at the same place from the regulatory direction, arguing that high-risk obligations are engineering work: automatic logging, six months of retention under your control, human oversight, post-market monitoring on production traces [6].

Our interpretation: the autonomy ladder and the provenance requirement are two views of one harness component. You cannot promote an agent up the ladder without the preserved record that justifies the promotion, and that record has to be designed before the first trace, not retrofitted [8].

## Where this is weak

The ownership material is framework and experience, not measurement. Grant presents an illustrative M&A case, Gayle a hypothetical bank workflow, and Arize's guide is vendor-authored with a named infrastructure partner. We have no evidence this month from any team that tried these changes and reported back, so every recommendation above is a hypothesis sourced from someone else's context. Nobody measured whether exception-only review actually degrades the way Grant predicts. Missing entirely: enterprise operators outside the US, and open-source maintainers.

## What to watch

Watch whether orchestration logic now moving into the model stays there. Anthropic's dynamic workflows, covered by Latent Space on 12 October, let Claude write its own orchestration program so developers do not manage the interconnections; Microsoft's 16 September skills-over-MCP post frames the same shift as giving the current reasoner a competence rather than delegating to another reasoner [9]. Both vendors sell the removal of developer-managed glue. Whether that glue stays gone — or reappears as configuration you cannot inspect — is the question for the next edition. Also watch for anyone replicating this month's measurement-grade results with an independent harness.
