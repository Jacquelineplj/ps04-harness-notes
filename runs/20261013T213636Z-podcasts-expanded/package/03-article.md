# What To Build Next: State Ownership, Staged Autonomy, and the Audit Trail You Don't Have Yet

The practical mandate from this month is to make your harness produce evidence by default: log enough to reconstruct a decision after the fact, and gate autonomy on measured override behaviour rather than on a passing demo. The useful accident: the cheapest version of compliance and the most useful version of debuggability are the same build.

## The evidence layer is one investment, not three

Three unrelated sources this month converge on the same logging schema. Paden Gayle, speaking at the AI Engineer event on deploying inside a bank, said at 12:49 that teams should preserve the input prompts, model version, configuration, output, reviewer and action — explicitly because "calling the model again later may produce a different answer," so you preserve what actually happened rather than trying to regenerate it [1].

Arize's engineering guide this month frames nearly the same list as regulatory work: most EU AI Act high-risk obligations are things you build — automatic logs, six months of retention, human oversight, post-market monitoring — and they take effect on 2 December 2027 [2]. Note the incentive: Arize sells the tracing stack it positions as the compliance evidence. We still read the convergence as real: the requirement is the same whether a regulator or a debugging engineer is asking.

The implication is narrow: if your traces record what the agent did but not the version and configuration that produced it, you have a log, not a record [1].

## Stage autonomy, and distrust your override rate

Michele Grant's talk sketches a four-stage ladder — shadow running, supervised draft, exception ensemble, then exceptions only — and the interesting part is the warning on the top rung. At 12:36 and 12:59 she argues that once humans review only exceptions, your biggest vulnerability is what the agent failed to flag, and that a falling override rate does not automatically mean the agent improved; it can equally mean the depth of review has slipped [3]. Her instrument is independent sampling, because the override rate alone cannot distinguish the two.

This is the governance-side version of the failure mode the second piece in this series found in benchmark grading: a success signal that certifies work which did not happen. Grant presents the ladder as a framework, without a measured outcome from running it.

## The economics constrain the autonomy plan

Steve Jamison of Jellyfish presented workflow data joined with AI telemetry showing AI at roughly 2.5% of the intelligence cost of producing a merged PR, with the dollar cost per merged PR falling 26% over a year across 83% of the companies studied [4]. Read straightforwardly, that argues for spending more on tokens.

Soukaïna Abelhad's due-diligence talk supplies the constraint. At 1:48 she argues the honest denominator must count everyone whose work exists because of AI — operators, reviewers, trainers, escalation and rework — and at 2:38 she separates "AI attributed" from genuinely autonomous completion [5]. Put together, our reading is that they bound each other: the case for more tokens holds only to the degree that the displaced human verification burden is counted honestly, and exception-only review is precisely the regime that hides it [6]. The teams best positioned next quarter will build the evidence layer first; it is the only thing that tells those two stories apart.

## Where this argument is weak

Grant's ladder and Abelhad's checks are practitioner frameworks with no quantitative validation attached [3][5]. The Jellyfish numbers are observational vendor telemetry from a company selling workflow analytics [4]. The AI Act guidance is vendor-authored, and Arize itself notes the deadlines have already moved once [2]. The strongest objection is one nothing here answers: heavy evidence infrastructure is a real cost, and for teams running low-consequence agents it may never pay back. No source we found measures that break-even point. Also absent: non-EU regulators, open-source maintainers who cannot fund a tracing layer, and anyone who has run a staged ladder to stage four and reported back.

## What to watch

Secret handling at the harness boundary. On 12 October Simon Willison published a small tool — a web editor served over Tailscale — so he could edit a credentials file without round-tripping it through the LLM [7]. Workarounds like this signal that the primitive is missing from the harness.

Composition-level red-teaming. The X-AgentRed paper, posted to arXiv on 9 October, reports 1.77x the harmful path rate of STAC and 2.99x its tool coverage across four frontier agents, with harm emerging from benign requests composing across services [8]. If that result replicates, per-tool permission prompts are the wrong unit of control.

Tool-surface strategy. On the MLOps Community podcast, an AWS practitioner noted at 30:48 that AWS has something like 16,000 APIs — far too many to model as tools — pushing toward code-mode execution, which he conceded at 15:01 is genuinely hard for a human to review [9]. Watch for a constrained middle layer that is both expressive and reviewable.

## What this means for builders

Ship the record before you ship the autonomy. Capture prompt, version, configuration, output and reviewer action on every run [1]; stage autonomy deliberately and instrument it with independent sampling rather than trusting the override rate [3]; and treat token-cost optimism as conditional on counting the humans it displaces [6].
