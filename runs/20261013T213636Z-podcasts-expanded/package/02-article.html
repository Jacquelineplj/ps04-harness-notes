Which Claims Held Up: Harnesses, Judges, and the Gap Between Passing and Working

The strongest results published this month are negative or conditional, and they converge on one uncomfortable finding: our standard success signals — passing tests, clean tool calls, a single good run — routinely certify work that did not happen. Where sources seem to disagree about harness costs, the disagreement dissolves into one conditional; the genuine conflicts are about LLM judges and whether "self-improvement" transfers at all.

Three results, one shape

Naresh Jain published a harness experiment on martinfowler.com on 13 October. He compared a coding agent building the same API service with and without a behavioral harness — architectural contracts plus deterministic test feedback during development. For the frontier model, the harness used about 10.5 times as many tokens, took nearly three times as long, and cost about seven times more, and both conditions passed all 178 acceptance tests in all ten runs [1]. The same harness took a cost-optimized model from 3 of 10 accepted runs to 10 of 10, at 33% lower cost per run than the unharnessed frontier model [2].

ThinkingBox's benchmark write-up, posted to Hugging Face on 3 October, attacks the same gap from the measurement side. It reports that single-attempt accuracy and every-attempt reliability rank models differently: Kimi-K3 solves 93.89% of tasks at least once but only 13.41% on all 20 attempts, while Claude Opus 5 solves fewer at least once and 47.53% consistently [3]. Claude Opus 5.5 scored higher than Opus 5 on every-attempt average and solved more tasks at least once, yet passed exactly the same 241 tasks on all 20 attempts [4].

The Daytona snapshot paper (arXiv, 9 October) is the cleanest negative of the three because it was pre-registered. The authors hypothesized that branching from promising sandbox snapshots would beat independent retry under a fixed token budget; on their harder Terminal-Bench subset, retry solved 17/25 task-seeds and snapshot search solved 6/25 [5]. Perfect state restoration was not the bottleneck. Selection was.

The apparent disagreement is one conditional

Read separately, these look like three verdicts on harness machinery. Read together, we think they are one conditional: added machinery pays when it supplies information the model or the repository does not already have, and costs when it does not [9]. Jain states a version of this himself — what changes with model capability is not whether independent sensors are needed but when they run [9]. The snapshot paper draws the same boundary from the other direction: full-machine restoration matters most when progress lives outside the repository, in databases, installed packages, or running services [9]. Microsoft's September post on distributed skills over MCP makes the parallel point about context, noting that a smaller initial context is not automatically a cheaper conversation [9]. That is interpretation on our part, not a claim any single source makes.

The real conflict is about judges and memory

Arize published a re-run of a widely-circulated tone experiment on 13 October. The original comparison confounded tone with brevity instructions — the rude prompts also told the model not to waste time — and when Arize deleted only the brevity words, Opus's apparent 55% saving fell to 15% and GPT-4o's threatening saving vanished entirely [6]. Arize sells eval tooling, so read the incentive; the method is checkable regardless.

Two independent Arize posts then converge on the LLM judge being the weak link. Their 12 October Harbor walkthrough says plainly that the judge "looked like the easy part. It was wrong a lot of the time," because TRAIL names the same tool page_down in one place and PageDownTool in another [7]. The tone post cites a RecSys '26 paper finding that a rude wrapper raised a judge's agreement with human annotators from 0.43 to about 0.48 — not by judging better, but by shifting its severity operating point [7]. Agreement went up; validity did not.

Against that, MC-EvoBench (arXiv, 9 October) was built specifically to audit experience-driven self-improvement, and reports that related experience yielded no stable transfer: it improved neither tool probe, its small wood-species gain hinged on one seed, and all intervals included zero [8]. That is a measured counterweight to industry confidence in memory-driven agents.

Where this evidence is thin

Jain's is ten paired runs on one greenfield Java service with an unusually detailed brief, and he says so; it cannot separate guides from sensors because both were added together [2]. ThinkingBox's tasks are synthetic reconstructions and its costs are list-price estimates [3]. MC-EvoBench rests on five seeds per probe with wide intervals, and its own authors note that early wood-collection and crafting bottlenecks often left later capabilities unobserved [8] — the absence of transfer may be partly an absence of measurement. None of this has been independently replicated. Absent entirely: security researchers on whether state-grading benchmarks can be gamed, and any enterprise operator reporting these effects at production scale.

What this means for builders

Stop treating a single passing run as evidence. If your agent touches real records, measure every-attempt reliability, not pass@k [3]. If you are adding harness machinery, ask what information it supplies that the model lacks — and be prepared for the answer "none" on frontier models [1]. And before you optimize against an LLM judge, read its decisions against the traces [7].
