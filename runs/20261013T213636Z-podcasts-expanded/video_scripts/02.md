# Which Claims Held Up: Harnesses, Judges, and the Gap Between Passing and Working

Runtime target: about 4 minutes 15 seconds (roughly 620 narration words)

**1. Hook**

Visual: Dark slide, single line of white text. Behind it, a slow b-roll loop of a terminal printing green "PASSED" lines.

On-screen text: Passing tests certify work that did not happen

Narration: The strongest agent results published this month are negative or conditional, and they all point at the same uncomfortable thing: our standard success signals — passing tests, clean tool calls, one good run — routinely certify work that did not actually happen. Three measured results landed within days of each other; read together they settle the argument about whether harness machinery is worth its cost. The real conflicts are elsewhere: LLM judges, and whether agent self-improvement transfers at all.

**2. Naresh Jain's harness experiment**

Visual: Quote card — "Thoughtworks, 'Are Harnesses Worth the Cost?', 13 October 2026." Below it, a two-bar chart labelled "Frontier model: tokens" with a short bar and a bar ten times longer.

On-screen text: 10.5× tokens. 7× cost. Same 10/10.

Narration: Naresh Jain published a harness experiment on martinfowler.com on the thirteenth of October. He had a coding agent build the same API service twice — once with a behavioral harness of architectural contracts and deterministic test feedback, once without. For the frontier model, the harness burned about ten and a half times as many tokens, took nearly three times as long, and cost about seven times more — and both conditions passed all ten runs. [02-c1] He reports being surprised; this contradicted his starting hypothesis. [02-c1]

**3. The same harness on a cheaper model**

Visual: Same chart style, now labelled "Cost-optimized model: accepted runs." Bar moves from 3 to 10. Caption: "33% lower cost per run than unharnessed frontier."

On-screen text: 3 of 10 → 10 of 10

Narration: Then the same harness on a cost-optimized model took it from three of ten accepted runs to ten of ten — at thirty-three percent lower cost per run than the unharnessed frontier model. [02-c2] Same machinery, opposite verdict. Worth noting: this is ten paired runs on one greenfield Java service with an unusually detailed brief, and Jain says so himself; it also cannot separate the guides from the sensors, because both were added together. [02-c2]

**4. ThinkingBox: pass@1 versus every attempt**

Visual: Side-by-side bars. Kimi-K3: 93.89% at least once / 13.41% all twenty. Claude Opus 5: lower first bar / 47.53% all twenty. Source strip: "ThinkingBox via Hugging Face, 3 October 2026."

On-screen text: Two leaderboards, two different winners

Narration: ThinkingBox attacks the same gap from the measurement side. It reports that single-attempt accuracy and every-attempt reliability rank models differently: Kimi-K3 solves nearly ninety-four percent of tasks at least once, but only thirteen percent on all twenty attempts, while Claude Opus 5 solves fewer at least once and forty-seven percent consistently. [02-c3] Sharper still — Claude Opus 5.5 scored higher on average than Opus 5, and passed exactly the same two hundred and forty-one tasks on all twenty attempts. [02-c4] Caveat: these tasks are synthetic reconstructions and the costs are list-price estimates. [02-c3]

---

**5. The pre-registered negative**

Visual: Clean scoreboard graphic. "Independent retry: 17/25. Snapshot branching: 6/25." Footer: "arXiv, 9 October 2026 — pre-registered."

On-screen text: Retry 17/25. Snapshots 6/25.

Narration: The Daytona snapshot paper is the cleanest of the three, because it was pre-registered. The authors expected that branching from promising sandbox snapshots would beat plain independent retry under a matched token budget. On their harder Terminal-Bench subset, retry solved seventeen of twenty-five task-seeds and snapshot search solved six. [02-c5] Perfect state restoration was never the bottleneck. Selection was.

---

**6. The disagreement that dissolves**

Visual: Three source logos converging into one text box reading: "Does it supply information the model or repo lacks?"

On-screen text: One conditional, not three verdicts

Narration: Read separately, those look like three competing verdicts on harness machinery. We think they are one conditional: added machinery pays when it supplies information the model or the repository does not already have, and costs when it does not. [02-c9] Jain says a version of this himself — what changes with model capability is not whether independent sensors are needed, but when they run. The snapshot paper draws the same line from the other side: full-machine restoration matters most when progress lives outside the repository, in databases, packages, or running services. [02-c9] That synthesis is ours, not any single source's.

---

**7. The judge problem**

Visual: Quote card — "Arize, 12 October 2026: 'The LLM judge looked like the easy part. It was wrong a lot of the time.'" Beside it, `page_down` versus `PageDownTool` in monospace.

On-screen text: Agreement went up. Validity did not.

Narration: Here is where sources conflict. Arize re-ran a widely circulated tone experiment and found the original confounded tone with brevity instructions. Delete only the brevity words, and Opus's apparent fifty-five percent saving falls to fifteen, and GPT-4o's threatening saving vanishes entirely. [02-c6] Then two independent Arize posts converge on the judge being the weak link: one says the judge was wrong a lot of the time because one tool is named two ways, the other cites a study where a rude wrapper shifted a judge's severity, not its judgement. [02-c7] Arize sells eval tooling — read the incentive; the method is checkable regardless.

---

**8. Memory's missing evidence**

Visual: Slide: "MC-EvoBench, arXiv, 9 October 2026 — built to audit self-improvement." Below: "No stable transfer. One seed. All intervals include zero."

On-screen text: No stable transfer

Narration: And against the industry's confidence in memory-driven agents: MC-EvoBench was built specifically to audit experience-driven self-improvement, and reports that related experience yielded no stable transfer — it improved neither tool probe, its one small gain hinged on a single seed, and all confidence intervals included zero. [02-c8] But that rests on five seeds per probe with wide intervals, and the authors note that most runs ended before later capabilities were ever observed. [02-c8] The absence of transfer may be partly an absence of measurement.

---

**9. Takeaway and limits**

Visual: Three-line checklist slide, then end card with the article title and "Sources linked in description."

On-screen text: Measure every-attempt. Audit your judge.

Narration: So: stop treating a single passing run as evidence. If your agent touches real records, measure every-attempt reliability rather than pass@k. [02-c3] If you are adding harness machinery, ask what information it supplies that the model lacks, and be ready for the answer "none" on a frontier model. [02-c1] And before you optimise against an LLM judge, read its decisions against the traces. [02-c7] Honest limitation: none of this has been independently replicated, and no enterprise operator has reported these effects at production scale. Full article and every source is linked in the description.
