# What To Build Next: State Ownership, Staged Autonomy, and the Audit Trail You Don't Have Yet

**Runtime target:** ~4 minutes (about 610 narration words)

---

## 1. Hook

**Visual:** Title card on dark background. Below it, three small icons in a row: a log file, a ladder, a dollar sign.

**On-screen text:** Ship the record before you ship the autonomy.

**Narration:** Here's the argument in one breath: the thing your harness most needs this month isn't a smarter loop, it's evidence by default. Log enough to reconstruct a decision after the fact, and gate autonomy on measured override behaviour rather than on a demo that passed. The useful accident is that the cheapest version of regulatory compliance and the most useful version of agent debuggability turn out to be the same build.

---

## 2. The logging schema, arrived at twice

**Visual:** Quote card. "Calling the model again later may produce a different answer." Attribution line: Paden Gayle, AI Engineer talk, 13 October 2026, at 12:49.

**On-screen text:** Prompt. Version. Config. Output. Reviewer. Action.

**Narration:** Paden Gayle, speaking at an AI Engineer event about deploying agents inside a bank, said about twelve and a half minutes in that teams should preserve the input prompts, the model version, the configuration, the output, the reviewer and the action — explicitly because calling the model again later may produce a different answer, so you preserve what actually happened. [03-c1] That's a reproducibility argument, not a compliance one.

---

## 3. The same list, from the regulator's side

**Visual:** Split slide. Left: "Gayle — reproducibility." Right: "Arize — EU AI Act." A bracket joining them labelled "same schema." Date badge: 2 December 2027.

**On-screen text:** Same schema. Two reasons. One build.

**Narration:** Arize, in a practical engineering guide published this month, frames nearly the same list as regulatory work: most EU AI Act high-risk obligations are things you build — automatic logs, six months of retention, human oversight, post-market monitoring — and they take effect on the second of December, 2027. [03-c2] Worth naming the incentive: Arize sells the tracing stack it positions as the compliance evidence. We read the convergence as real anyway, because the requirement is identical whether a regulator or a debugging engineer is asking. If your traces record what the agent did but not the version and configuration that produced it, you have a log, not a record. [03-c1]

---

## 4. The ladder, and the trap at the top

**Visual:** Four-rung ladder graphic: shadow, supervised draft, exception ensemble, exceptions only. The top rung highlighted red.

**On-screen text:** A falling override rate is not proof.

**Narration:** Michele Grant's talk sketches a four-stage autonomy ladder — shadow running, supervised draft, exception ensemble, then exceptions only. The interesting part is the warning on the top rung. Around twelve and a half minutes in, she argues that once humans review only exceptions, your biggest vulnerability is what the agent failed to flag — and that a falling override rate does not automatically mean the agent improved. It can equally mean the depth of review has slipped. [03-c3] Her instrument is independent sampling, because the override rate alone cannot tell those apart. She presents this as a framework, with no measured outcome from running it.

---

## 5. Tokens are cheap — conditionally

**Visual:** Simple bar chart described: one bar at 2.5% labelled AI, one at 97.5% labelled salary. Caption: Jellyfish, August 2026 data.

**On-screen text:** AI ≈ 2.5% of cost per merged PR.

**Narration:** Steve Jamison of Jellyfish presented workflow data joined with AI telemetry showing AI at roughly two and a half percent of the intelligence cost of producing a merged pull request, with dollar cost per merged PR falling twenty-six percent over a year across eighty-three percent of companies studied. [03-c4] Read straightforwardly, that's an argument for spending more on tokens.

---

## 6. The constraint

**Visual:** Quote card: "Count everyone whose work exists because of AI." Attribution: Soukaïna Abelhad, AI Engineer talk, 13 October 2026.

**On-screen text:** Operators. Reviewers. Trainers. Escalation. Rework.

**Narration:** Soukaïna Abelhad's due-diligence talk supplies the brake. She argues the honest denominator must count everyone whose work exists because of AI — operators, reviewers, trainers, escalation and rework — and she separates work that is merely "AI attributed" from genuinely autonomous completion. [03-c5] We think these two findings bound each other: the case for spending more on tokens holds only to the degree that the human verification burden it displaces is counted honestly, and exception-only review is precisely the regime that makes that burden hard to observe. [03-c6]

---

## 7. Three things to watch

**Visual:** Three stacked rows with source badges — "Simon Willison, 12 Oct", "arXiv, 9 Oct", "MLOps Community podcast, 28 Sep".

**On-screen text:** Secrets. Composition. Tool surface.

**Narration:** Three things next quarter. Simon Willison published a small web editor served over Tailscale on the twelfth of October, specifically so he could edit a credentials file without round-tripping those credentials through the LLM — a narrow workaround that signals a missing harness primitive. [03-c7] Second, the X-AgentRed paper, posted to arXiv on the ninth, reports one-point-seven-seven times the harmful path rate of STAC and nearly three times its tool coverage across four frontier agents, with harm emerging from benign requests composing across services. [03-c8] If that replicates, per-tool permission prompts are the wrong unit of control. Third, on the MLOps Community podcast, about thirty minutes in, an AWS practitioner notes AWS has something like sixteen thousand APIs — far too many to model as tools — pushing toward code-mode execution that he concedes is genuinely hard to review. [03-c9]

---

## 8. Takeaway and the honest limit

**Visual:** Closing slide, three bullets. Faded panel at bottom reading "Unmeasured: does the evidence layer pay back for low-consequence agents?"

**On-screen text:** Build the record first. Then raise the ladder.

**Narration:** So: capture prompt, version, configuration, output and reviewer action on every run. [03-c1] Stage autonomy deliberately and instrument it with independent sampling rather than trusting the override rate. [03-c3] And treat token-cost optimism as conditional on counting the humans it displaces. [03-c6] Here's the honest limit. Grant's ladder and Abelhad's checks are practitioner frameworks with no quantitative validation attached. The Jellyfish numbers are observational vendor telemetry. The AI Act guidance is vendor-authored, and Arize itself notes the deadlines have already moved once. [03-c2] And the strongest objection is one nothing here answers: heavy evidence infrastructure is a real cost, and for low-consequence agents it may never pay back. Nobody we found has measured that break-even. The full article and every source are linked in the description.
