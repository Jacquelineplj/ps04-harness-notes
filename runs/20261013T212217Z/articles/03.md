# What To Build Next: Grading Surfaces, Reliability Layers, and the Approval Ladder

Three changes to your harness follow from this month's evidence: move your pass/fail signal from the agent's trajectory to the terminal state of the system it touched, insert a reliability layer between tool retrieval and tool execution, and turn autonomy from a boolean into a ladder with explicit evidence gates. Each is backed by a specific measured result or a documented practitioner framework from this window, and all three attack the same weakness — the agent's self-report of success is the least trustworthy signal you have.

## Change the grading surface

The ThinkingBox post on Hugging Face, dated 3 October, makes a claim that is easy to skip past: the terminal-state check its benchmark grades on is also available as a production signal, so you should verify end state before committing rather than trusting the model's summary [03-c1]. That is a harness instruction, not a benchmark result. It matters because the same source attributes roughly four in five failures to tool handling rather than reasoning, which reframes error recovery as a harness problem before it is a model problem [03-c2].

We read the implication as uncomfortable for most production gates. If your release criterion is "the agent finished and reported no error," and failures cluster in tool handling rather than reasoning [03-c2], your gate is measuring the part of the system least likely to break. The fix ThinkingBox proposes — check the records left behind — costs an executable assertion per workflow, not a research program [03-c1].

## Add a reliability layer under retrieval

The RATED paper, posted to arXiv on 9 October, crawled tool catalogs from four major agent platforms and reports that over half the tools carry critical flaws such as broken links and missing authentication [03-c3]. If that holds for catalogs resembling yours, semantic retrieval is selecting from a pool where failure is common and not visible in the documentation.

RATED's proposed remedy is a bandit that re-ranks retrieval candidates on live execution feedback. The authors report cutting fault selection rate from 86.2% to 2.9% under a 30% API failure rate, with under 1ms of added latency [03-c4]. Taken at face value, that is the cheapest intervention in this month's evidence by a wide margin. It is also the one we would most want to see replicated before betting on it.

## Treat the tool catalog as attack surface

X-AgentRed, also posted 9 October, reports discovering harmful cross-service paths at 1.77x the rate of its baseline while covering 2.99x as many distinct tools [03-c5]. The practical reading: compositional risk scales with the size of your connected catalog, not with how carefully you wrote your system prompt. Every MCP server you add expands the space of benign-looking tool compositions that reach a harmful end state.

## Formalise the approval ladder

Two AI Engineer talks published 13 October describe the same four-rung structure. Paden Gayle's talk, at 9:12, reads the ladder bottom-up: suggest, draft, acts-with-approval, acts, with promotion driven by collected evidence rather than confidence [03-c7]. At 12:49 he names the record required to reconstruct a consequential decision — prompt, model version, configuration, output, reviewer action [03-c7].

Michele Grant's talk supplies the warning that matters most for harness builders. At 12:36 she says that once humans review only exceptions, the biggest vulnerability is what the agent failed to flag; at 12:59 she adds that a falling override rate can mean review depth slipped rather than the agent improved [03-c6]. That is a metric most teams would read backwards.

Arize's 13 October engineering guide argues that EU AI Act high-risk obligations — automatic logging, six months of retention, human oversight, post-market monitoring — are mostly engineering work overlapping with good production practice, with a December 2027 deadline [03-c8]. The overlap with Gayle's evidence pack is near-total [03-c7].

Across ThinkingBox, both talks, and Arize's review tooling, we read a single converging principle: the agent's own report of success is the weakest signal in the system, so the harness must supply independent verification at every rung [03-c9].

## Where this evidence is thin

These recommendations rest on evidence of very different weight. RATED's 86.2%-to-2.9% figure is a single unreplicated paper using injected failures on a synthetic protocol; the authors list live-runtime validation as future work [03-c4]. X-AgentRed's harmful-path rates depend on LLM judges whose disagreement the paper quantifies — accepting either judge rather than requiring agreement moves reported rates by 15 to 30 points [03-c5]. The autonomy-ladder talks are case narratives, one explicitly framed as hypothetical, with no measured outcomes [03-c6][03-c7]. And the EU AI Act guidance is vendor-authored by a company selling the tracing product that would satisfy the obligation; it says plainly it is engineering guidance, not legal advice [03-c8]. We have no independent enterprise operator confirming any of these patterns at scale.

## What to watch

Three things. Whether RATED-style reliability re-ranking appears in any production MCP client, or stays a paper [03-c4]. Whether snapshot-based state restoration finds a selection heuristic — the snapshot study names identifying which machine states represent reusable progress as its main open problem, and that, not restoration mechanics, is the blocker [03-c10]. And whether vendors shipping self-planning orchestration also ship terminal-state verification, or leave that to customers [03-c1]. Watch the catalog-size trend too: if tool counts keep climbing, X-AgentRed's coverage finding says your exposure grows with them whether or not you changed anything else [03-c5].
