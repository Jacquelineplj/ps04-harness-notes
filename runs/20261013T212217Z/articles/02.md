# Which Claims Held Up: Measured Results, Negative Results, and Numbers Without Methods

The most trustworthy evidence this month is negative: a snapshot system that lost to plain retry, an experience-transfer benchmark that found nothing stable, and a replication that dissolved its own headline. The most-shared numbers, by contrast, arrive as assertions — throughput figures, decision-model accuracies, and agent-team gains whose methods are not on the page.

## The negative results are the sturdy ones

The Daytona snapshot paper, posted to arXiv on 9 October, is the clearest example. Its broad hypothesis — that reliable environment restoration improves test-time scaling for coding agents — did not survive its own experiment. What makes the paper useful is the condition it then isolates: on a custom task whose progress lives in a SQLite database outside git, full snapshots solved 4 of 5 attempts and source-only restoration solved 0 of 5 [02-c2]. That is a narrow, actionable boundary. The authors also name the mechanism they did not solve, writing that snapshotting addresses state restoration but not state selection, and that effective environment-level search needs both [02-c10].

MC-EvoBench lands in the same shape. It reports no stable transfer from related experience: every confidence interval it reports includes zero, and the single positive contrast hinges on one seed [02-c3]. We read that as a result worth more than a positive number with no error bars, precisely because the authors declined to round it up.

Arize's 13 October post is the month's cleanest methodological correction. Replicating a claim that rude prompts save tokens, Arize found that most of the apparent saving was a confound with brevity instructions rather than tone, and that accuracy did not move across tones on any of the six models tested [02-c5]. The original prompts asked rude models for short answers and polite models for nothing in particular. This is not a disagreement of opinion; it is one experiment finding a flaw in another's design. Arize sells eval tooling, so the incentive runs toward "your evals are subtly broken" — but the piece argues against its own first result, which we take as evidence of good faith.

## Numbers reported, not shown

The 10 October Latent Space roundup carries LangChain's claim that routing each task to the cheapest adequate model cut median Open SWE cost per task by 64%, and Perplexity's claim of 94.5% Decision Bench accuracy across 1,071 cases — neither accompanied by a description of method [02-c6]. These may be fine. They are, as retained, asserted rather than shown, and our characterisation reflects what the secondary source contains, not a judgement about the underlying work.

The same roundup contains a figure that cuts the other way. An independent evaluation of GPT-6 Sol and Opus 5.5 on Vibe Code Bench, alone and as teams, found teams cost 1.8–5.1x more with only one configuration improving significantly [02-c7]. That sits awkwardly against the enthusiasm for sub-agent fan-out, including Anthropic's own framing at its dynamic-workflows session, where the team said flows cost "much more" than a single-agent approach but were "much more effective at finding all of the details" [02-c7].

## Where the tension is real

Steve Jamison of Jellyfish, speaking at AI Engineer on 13 October, reported cross-company telemetry showing the dollar cost of a merged PR fell 26% over the last year alongside a 60x increase in AI spend. At 10:23 he addressed quality directly, saying revert-rate deltas are "just slightly below the noise in the data" and "not evidence of a improvement here" [02-c8]. Jellyfish sells engineering analytics, so the throughput finding runs with its commercial interest — but the speaker drew his own boundary, which is more than most vendor decks do. The honest reading is that the cost correlation is observational and real, and the quality conclusion is simply not yet supported.

A practitioner report from the same week shows a different failure surface. In a cloning competition judged first by an automated business eval, the top seven entrants landed within 2.3 points of each other — saturation — while human reviewers rated six of the AI judge's top ten as weak, and the eventual winner placed 19th on the automated leaderboard [02-c9]. The organisers note some divergence was intentional. Still, if your harness hill-climbs a grader, this is what saturation looks like before anyone checks.

## What we cannot tell you

Every measured result here is a single study with no independent replication. The snapshot paper, MC-EvoBench, Arize's rerun, and the Thoughtworks harness experiment — which covered 10 paired runs per model on one greenfield Java service and says plainly that the sample "cannot establish long-run reliability" [02-c10] — are each one team's method. The negative results are as un-replicated as the positive ones. Missing entirely: any enterprise operator publishing independent numbers against the vendor claims, and any security researcher replicating this month's red-teaming work.

## What this means for builders

Adopt the conditional findings and ignore the unconditioned ones. Snapshot your sandboxes only where progress escapes version control; check whether your prompt-efficiency wins are tone or just brevity; price sub-agent fan-out against a single-agent baseline before you ship it. A due-diligence talk at AI Engineer put the test well: the job is not to ask whether a number is impressive, but what must be true for it to matter [02-c10].
