# Which Claims Held Up: The Month's Best Evidence Was Negative

Runtime target: 4 minutes 15 seconds (approximately 640 narration words)

**Visual:** Presenter on camera. Behind them, a split slide: left panel in green reads "Stated its failure condition"; right panel in grey reads "Reported a number only."

**On-screen text:** The best evidence this month was negative

**Narration:** The most trustworthy results in agent engineering this month were the ones that failed. A snapshot system that lost to plain retry. A transfer benchmark that found nothing stable. A replication that dissolved its own headline. Meanwhile the numbers everyone shared — throughput figures, decision-model accuracies, agent-team gains — arrived without methods. Our argument is simple: the claims that survived scrutiny this month are the ones whose authors told you where they break. [02-c10]

**2.**

**Visual:** Quote card styled as an arXiv paper header. Title: "Returning to Development States: When Full Sandbox Snapshots Help Coding Agents." Publisher line: "arXiv, 9 October 2026." Below it, a simple two-bar chart: "Full snapshots: 4 of 5 solved" against "Git-diff restoration: 0 of 5 solved."

**On-screen text:** Snapshots: 4/5. Source-only restore: 0/5.

**Narration:** Start with the Daytona snapshot paper, posted to arXiv on the ninth of October. Its broad hypothesis — that reliable environment restoration improves test-time scaling for coding agents — did not survive its own experiment. But then it isolates the narrow case where snapshots do win: on a task whose progress lives in a SQLite database outside git, full snapshots solved four of five attempts and source-only restoration solved zero of five. [02-c2] That is a boundary you can actually act on.

**3.**

**Visual:** Slide with three stacked rows, each a horizontal confidence-interval bar crossing a vertical zero line. Caption underneath: "MC-EvoBench: every reported interval includes zero."

**On-screen text:** No stable transfer. All intervals include zero.

**Narration:** MC-EvoBench lands in the same shape. It reports no stable transfer from related experience: every confidence interval it reports includes zero, and the single positive contrast hinges on one seed. [02-c3] We think that is worth more than a positive number with no error bars — precisely because the authors declined to round it up.

**4.**

**Visual:** Quote card. Publisher line: "Arize, 13 October 2026 — 'Does being rude to AI work? What 6 models revealed.'" Below, two numbers with arrows: "Opus very-rude saving: 55% → 15%" and "GPT-4o threatening saving: 22% → none."

**On-screen text:** It was brevity, not rudeness

**Narration:** The cleanest methodological correction of the month comes from Arize. Replicating the claim that rude prompts save tokens, Arize reports that most of the apparent saving was a confound with brevity instructions rather than tone — and that accuracy did not move across tones on any of the six models tested. [02-c5] Delete only the brevity words and the rudeness discount largely vanishes. That is not a disagreement of opinion. It is one experiment finding a flaw in another's design. Arize sells eval tooling, so the incentive runs toward "your evals are subtly broken" — but the piece argues against its own first result, which we read as good faith.

**5.**

**Visual:** Slide titled "Reported, not shown." Two bullet lines on a grey card: "LangChain: median Open SWE cost per task down 64%" and "Perplexity: 94.5% Decision Bench accuracy, 1,071 cases." A footer strip reads "Source: Latent Space roundup, 10 October 2026 — no method described."

**On-screen text:** Reported, not shown

**Narration:** Now the other column. The tenth of October Latent Space roundup carries LangChain's claim that routing each task to the cheapest adequate model cut median Open SWE cost per task by sixty-four percent, and Perplexity's claim of ninety-four-point-five percent Decision Bench accuracy — neither with a description of method. [02-c6] These may well be fine. As retained, they are asserted rather than shown.

**6.**

**Visual:** Same roundup styling, but the card is flipped to a cautionary colour. Text: "Agent teams: 1.8–5.1x cost. One configuration improved significantly."

**On-screen text:** Teams cost 1.8–5.1x more

**Narration:** The same roundup contains a figure cutting the other way. An independent evaluation of models alone and as teams found that agent teams cost one-point-eight to five-point-one times more, with only one configuration improving significantly. [02-c7] That sits awkwardly beside the enthusiasm for sub-agent fan-out — including Anthropic's own framing, where the team said flows cost "much more" than a single-agent approach but were "much more effective at finding all of the details." [02-c7]

**7.**

**Visual:** Quote card: "Steve Jamison, Jellyfish — AI Engineer, 13 October 2026." Pull quote in large type: "These deltas are just slightly below the noise in the data. It's not evidence of a improvement here." Sidebar: "Cost per merged PR: −26%. AI spend: 60x."

**On-screen text:** Cost down 26%. Quality: not shown.

**Narration:** Steve Jamison of Jellyfish, speaking at AI Engineer on the thirteenth of October, reported cross-company telemetry: the dollar cost of a merged pull request fell twenty-six percent over the last year, alongside a sixty-fold rise in AI spend. About ten minutes in, he addressed quality directly, saying the revert-rate deltas are just slightly below the noise and, in his words, not evidence of an improvement. [02-c8] Jellyfish sells engineering analytics, so the throughput finding runs with its commercial interest — but the speaker drew his own boundary. We read it as: the cost correlation is real and observational; the quality conclusion is not yet supported.

---

**8.**

**Visual:** Leaderboard graphic. Rows 1 through 7 clustered tightly with the label "within 2.3 points — saturation." A separate highlighted row at position 19 marked "Human reviewers' winner."

**On-screen text:** AI judge top 7 tied. Human winner: 19th.

**Narration:** One more failure surface, from a practitioner cloning competition reported this week. Judged first by an automated business eval, the top seven entrants landed within two-point-three points of each other — saturation — while human reviewers rated six of the AI judge's top ten as weak, and the eventual winner placed nineteenth on the automated leaderboard. [02-c9] If your harness hill-climbs a grader, that is what saturation looks like before anyone checks.

---

**9.**

**Visual:** Closing slide, three short lines: "Snapshot only where progress escapes git." / "Check if your win is tone or brevity." / "Price fan-out against a single-agent baseline." Lower third points to the article link.

**On-screen text:** Adopt the conditional. Ignore the unconditioned.

**Narration:** So: adopt the conditional findings, ignore the unconditioned ones. Snapshot sandboxes only where progress escapes version control. Check whether your prompt-efficiency win is tone or just brevity. Price sub-agent fan-out against a single-agent baseline before you ship it. The honest limitation: every measured result here is a single study with no independent replication — the negative results are as un-replicated as the positive ones, and no enterprise operator has published independent numbers against the vendor claims. [02-c10] A due-diligence talk at the same conference put the test well: the job is not to ask whether a number is impressive, but what must be true for it to matter. [02-c10] Full write-up and every source is linked in the description.
