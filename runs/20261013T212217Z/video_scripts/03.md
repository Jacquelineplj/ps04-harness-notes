# What To Build Next: Grading Surfaces, Reliability Layers, and the Approval Ladder

Runtime target: about 4 minutes 30 seconds (roughly 660 narration words)

## 1. Hook

**Visual:** Dark slide, large white text. Beneath it, a small two-line mock terminal: `agent: task complete ✓` above `db: 0 rows written`.

**On-screen text:** The agent says done. Check anyway.

**Narration:** This month's agent research points at one weakness, and it's not the model. The agent's own report that it succeeded is the least trustworthy signal in your whole system [03-c9]. Three changes follow from that: move your pass/fail check from the agent's trajectory to the terminal state of whatever it touched, put a reliability layer between tool retrieval and tool execution, and stop treating autonomy as a yes-or-no switch. Each one is backed by a specific measured result or a documented practitioner framework from the last two weeks. Let's go through them, and then through where the evidence is thin, because it is thin in places.

## 2. Change the grading surface

**Visual:** Quote card — "The same signal the benchmark grades on is available in production: check the terminal state before you commit, not the model's summary of it." Attribution line: ThinkingBox, via Hugging Face, 3 October 2026.

**On-screen text:** Grade the end state, not the summary

**Narration:** Start with grading. The ThinkingBox post, published on Hugging Face on the third of October, argues that the terminal-state check its benchmark grades on is also available as a production signal — so verify the end state before you commit, rather than trusting the model's summary [03-c1]. That's a harness instruction dressed up as a benchmark note. We think most production gates fail it. If your release criterion is "the agent finished and reported no error," you are measuring the thing least likely to break.

## 3. Failures are a harness problem

**Visual:** Simple stacked bar, one bar, split roughly 80 / 20. Large segment labelled "tool handling", small segment labelled "reasoning". Caption: ThinkingBox, 3 Oct 2026.

**On-screen text:** ~4 in 5 failures: tool handling

**Narration:** The same source attributes roughly four in five failures to tool handling rather than reasoning [03-c2]. That reframes error recovery as a harness problem before it's a model problem [03-c2]. And the fix they propose is cheap — one executable assertion per workflow that checks the records left behind, not a research programme [03-c1].

## 4. The tool catalog is broken

**Visual:** Slide with four generic platform tiles greyed out, and a red "50%+" badge across them. Source line: RATED, arXiv, 9 October 2026.

**On-screen text:** Over half of crawled tools: critical flaws

**Narration:** Second change: reliability under retrieval. The RATED paper, posted to arXiv on the ninth of October, crawled tool catalogs from four major agent platforms, and reports that over half the tools carry critical flaws — broken links, missing authentication [03-c3]. If that's true of catalogs resembling yours, your semantic retriever is picking from a pool where failure is common and invisible in the documentation [03-c3].

## 5. The cheapest intervention — with an asterisk

**Visual:** Big before/after number card: "86.2% → 2.9%" with small print "fault selection rate, 30% injected API failure rate, <1ms added latency". Source line: RATED, arXiv, 9 Oct 2026.

**On-screen text:** 86.2% → 2.9%, under 1ms

**Narration:** RATED's remedy is a bandit that re-ranks retrieval candidates on live execution feedback. The authors report cutting fault selection rate from eighty-six point two percent to two point nine percent under a thirty percent API failure rate, with under one millisecond of added latency [03-c4]. Taken at face value, that is by far the cheapest intervention in this month's evidence. It is also the one we'd most want replicated — it's a single paper, using injected failures on a synthetic protocol, and the authors themselves list live-runtime validation as future work [03-c4].

## 6. Your catalog is your attack surface

**Visual:** Node graph b-roll: a central agent node connecting to a growing ring of tool nodes, a few paths highlighted red. Caption card: X-AgentRed, arXiv, 9 October 2026.

**On-screen text:** 1.77x harmful paths. 2.99x tools touched.

**Narration:** Third, security. X-AgentRed, also posted on the ninth, reports discovering harmful cross-service paths at one point seven seven times the rate of its baseline, while covering two point nine nine times as many distinct tools [03-c5]. Our reading: compositional risk scales with the size of your connected catalog, not with how carefully you wrote your system prompt [03-c5]. Caveat — those harmful-path rates depend on LLM judges whose disagreement the paper itself quantifies; accepting either judge instead of requiring agreement moves the numbers by fifteen to thirty points [03-c5].

## 7. The approval ladder

**Visual:** Four-rung ladder graphic, bottom to top: Suggest / Draft / Acts with approval / Acts. Quote card beside it: "A falling override rate does not automatically mean the agent is getting smarter." — Michele Grant, AI Engineer talk, 13 October 2026.

**On-screen text:** Suggest. Draft. Approve. Act.

**Narration:** Two AI Engineer talks published on the thirteenth describe the same four rungs. In Paden Gayle's talk, about nine minutes in, he reads the ladder bottom-up — suggest, draft, acts-with-approval, acts — with promotion driven by collected evidence rather than confidence, and he names the record you need to reconstruct a decision: prompt, model version, configuration, output, reviewer action [03-c7]. Michele Grant's talk, around twelve and a half minutes in, supplies the warning that matters most: once humans review only exceptions, your biggest vulnerability is what the agent failed to flag, and a falling override rate can mean review depth slipped rather than the agent improved [03-c6]. Most teams would read that metric backwards.

## 8. The compliance overlap

**Visual:** Split slide. Left: Gayle's evidence list. Right: "Automatic logs · 6 months retention · Human oversight · Post-market monitoring · Dec 2027". Source line: Arize, 13 October 2026.

**On-screen text:** Mostly engineering work. Deadline Dec 2027.

**Narration:** Arize's engineering guide, same date, claims EU AI Act high-risk obligations — automatic logging, six months of retention, human oversight, post-market monitoring — are mostly engineering work that overlaps with good production practice, with a December twenty twenty-seven deadline [03-c8]. The overlap with Gayle's evidence pack is near-total [03-c7]. Worth flagging: that guide is vendor-authored by a company selling the tracing product that would satisfy the obligation, and it says plainly it's engineering guidance, not legal advice [03-c8].

## 9. What to watch, and what we don't know

**Visual:** Three-line watchlist slide, then a final card with the publication name and "Full article and every source linked in the description."

**On-screen text:** Three things to watch

**Narration:** So: three things to watch. Whether RATED-style reliability re-ranking shows up in any production MCP client, or stays a paper [03-c4]. Whether snapshot-based state restoration finds a selection heuristic — that study names identifying which machine states represent reusable progress as its main open problem, and selection, not restoration mechanics, is the blocker [03-c10]. And whether vendors shipping self-planning orchestration also ship terminal-state verification, or quietly leave that to you [03-c1]. The honest limitation: the autonomy-ladder material is case narrative with no measured outcomes, one of them explicitly framed as hypothetical [03-c6][03-c7], and we have no independent enterprise operator confirming any of these patterns at scale. Treat the direction as well-supported and the magnitudes as provisional. The full write-up, with every source linked, is in the description.
